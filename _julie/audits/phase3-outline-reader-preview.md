# Phase-3 Outline Reader Preview — Prospect-Eyes Pass

*Eleven chapter outlines, judged on face value (structure + reflection questions) by readers playing the ideal customer — the 25-to-100-person CEO running EOS or Scaling Up — before any drafting begins.*

---

## 1. Overall read

**These outlines are ready to draft from — with eyes open.** Not one of the eleven was judged structurally broken (no "No" verdicts); every chapter earns a "Mostly." The bones are sound: clean syllogisms, a Meridian thread that carries the worked example, artifacts the reader can actually fill in, and reflection questions that drive working sessions rather than reverie. What the readers flag is not "rethink the chapter" but "resolve a small set of recurring questions while drafting." The same handful of issues appears chapter after chapter — undefined jargon arriving before its definition, payoff sections positioned too late or trimmed too hard, forward-references that read as deferrals, and "so-what" beats that diagnose without prescribing a recovery path. None of these require an outline rewrite; all of them require the Drafter to hold a shared answer key. Treat this as a **green light with a pre-flight checklist**, not a blocked runway.

## 3. Consolidated question list for the author

These are the things a prospect would want answered. Deduped across chapters and grouped by theme. Resolve them before or during drafting — most are one-sentence fixes the Drafter can apply consistently once the author decides the answer.

### THEME A — Jargon that arrives before its definition (highest-frequency issue)
The reader hits a loaded technical term, gets no plain-English handle, and disengages. Decide the house rule: **every loaded term gets a one-sentence plain-English gloss at first use, even when the canonical definition lives in a later chapter.**

- **"Agent"** — first loaded use lands in Ch 1 §V and Ch 2 §IV.E ("feedstock for agents") with the full definition deferred to Ch 3/Ch 7. The "not a chatbot" placeholder won't hold. *(Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 3)*
- **"Indexed asset"** (Ch 1 §III WITH/WITHOUT list) — undefined, will stop a non-technical reader cold.
- **RAG, MCPs, APIs, connectors, data pipelines, service layers** — appear as decision inputs in Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 8 *before* the literacy content that defines them. Confirm the Phase-3B jargon sweep actually inserts inline definitions where first used. *(Ch 6 §XI, Ch 7 §VI, Ch 8 §VII/§XII)*
- **"Context engineering," "ontologies, embeddings, knowledge graphs," "Systems of Semantics"** (Ch 6 §III, §XIII) — need one-sentence analogies each or the CEO disengages.
- **"Orchestrator"** — EOS readers hear "Integrator." Every use of "orchestrator agent" (software) vs. "Human Orchestrator" (role) must disambiguate at the seam. *(Ch 3 §XII, Ch 7 §VIII, Ch 12 §IV)*
- **"Constraint Clarity," "the constraint workflow," "Sprint Planning Canvas," "Hybrid Org Today," "Sprint Outcome Record"** — terms used as if already known. Confirm each was introduced and named before it's leaned on. *(Ch 5 §V/§XI, Ch 6, Ch 7b §IV.G, Ch 11 §IV, Ch 12, Ch 13)*

### THEME B — Payoff / thesis sections positioned too late or trimmed too hard
The reader reaches the chapter's whole reason for existing and finds it buried, rushed, or following a document-description.

- **Ch 11 §XI (Compound bends the headcount slope)** — the book's central payoff, trimmed to 4–5 lines and sandwiched between a story and the close. Readers say this is backwards: it should be the last substantive move, with real estate to match.
- **Ch 12 §VII (Headcount Paradox resolved)** — the emotional payoff of the whole book, placed *after* a one-page-document description (§VI). Anticlimactic sequencing.
- **Ch 4 §VIII (compounding payoff "Run One Sprint. Then Another.")** — the most important claim lands at 95% depth where a fatigued reader will miss it.
- **Ch 2 §V.B (Shift B, math can bend)** — trimmed to a callback with the proof examples moved to Ch 1, leaving the shift theoretical with no worksheet row and no reflection question.

### THEME C — Macro-structure revealed after the detail it should precede
Several chapters walk the parts, *then* tell the reader how the parts split.

- **Ch 4 §VI (Diagnose vs. Execute & Compound split)** arrives *after* all six stages are walked. The split should preface the six-stage walk, not follow it.
- **Ch 6 §XI (what makes a source "Connected")** arrives *after* §X asks the reader to classify sources as Connected/Manual/Broken. Logic inversion — define before classify.
- **Ch 8 §XI (Span-of-Control)** — heading says "verify *before* you pick the environment" but it's positioned *after* the environment decision (§X). Fix the contradiction.
- **Ch 1 §VI.B (Technical vs. Operating-Model preconditions split)** — 2-bucket framework and 5-dimension list both presented as THE structure; clarify how the 2 relate to the 5 before scoring.

### THEME D — "So-what" beats that diagnose without prescribing
A section tells the reader they've failed a test, then doesn't tell them what to do about it.

- **Ch 11 §VI** — "if you can't answer these three, the Sprint didn't compound" — verdict but no recovery path, no Action Step follows.
- **Ch 5 §XIII (Activity Is Not Signal)** — describes the failure mode but the only instruction is "don't do that"; needs a redirect ("go run §XI").
- **Ch 7 §XI / Ch 7b §IX–X** — Bedard 3-agent ceiling exceeded, or supervisor fails Trace/Challenge/Apply — no concrete next move (split the seat? defer agents? train?). Give at least one option.
- **Ch 7b §XII (prototype "five responses")** — asserts the number, never says the mechanism (call? form? one-page mockup?).

### THEME E — Where does this fit in my existing EOS / Scaling Up rhythm?
The single most repeated operational question from the target reader.

- **When/where does a Signal session live** relative to my L10 / quarterly / IDS? Does it replace or layer? *(Ch 5 §XII, Ch 12 §V)*
- **Existing EOS Accountability Chart vs. the Hybrid Accountability Chart** — replace? add a layer? This is THE place to make the EOS connection explicit. *(Ch 4 §V.C, Ch 7, Ch 12)*
- **Does the Design Team fold into an existing seat (Integrator/COO) or is it a new meeting?** *(Ch 7 §XIII)*
- **Where does the Knowledge Manager role sit on my Accountability Chart in a 25–50-person company?** *(Ch 6 §XIV)*
- **Name the GWC parallel** (Sees It / Wants It / Suited for It ↔ Gets It / Wants It / Capacity) explicitly — readers see it and want it surfaced. *(Ch 7 §VII, Ch 13 §IX)*

### THEME F — The ERP / developer / coach assumptions that exclude segments of the reader
- **Ch 1 §IV** anchors RPE on "your last ERP implementation." Many 25–100-person agencies/services/distributors have no ERP — the table breaks for them. Provide an alternate anchor.
- **Ch 2 §IV.B / §VI** — systems inventory assumes CRM+ERP+PM+comms+docs. "What if my ERP is a spreadsheet / I only have two of these?" — needs a hedge.
- **"I don't have a developer"** — Ch 7 §XII (category 3 needs an engineer), Ch 8 §VI ("you don't need a developer") vs. §X/§XIV (Claude Code is dev-heavy; Meridian used a freelance dev for 3 days). Resolve the self-build-vs-contract-help decision rule explicitly.
- **"Compound coach"** (Ch 7 §VIII) — paid engagement, book resource, or something else? Trust-eroding if unresolved.

### THEME G — Timeline and effort honesty for a busy CEO
- **Ch 12 §VII** — "slope bends after 6–8 Sprints" = 18–24 months. Disclose plainly so a reader expecting one-quarter results doesn't set the book down.
- **Ch 5 §XI.A** — "no time budget, runs until the Statement is written." Give a ballpark ("most Signal sessions run 2–4 hours") or the calendar-packed CEO shuts down.
- **Ch 3 §IX / §XIII–XIV, Ch 7** — multiple Action Steps stacked in one chapter. Make the cumulative ask visible and give a selection criterion for "which role/constraint do I start with?"
- **Ch 12 §VI** — four agenda steps + updating a living document in a 1–2 hour session: show the time allocation.

### THEME H — Accumulating artifacts that read as disconnected worksheets
- Across Ch 2 / Ch 7 / Ch 7b, the reader fills an Information Flow Spec, HAC rows, autonomy placements, a worksheet — and isn't told until late that these accumulate into one **Design Brief**. Name the destination document early and confirm each worksheet feeds it.
- **Ch 2 §VI worksheet → Ch 3 vs. Ch 5** — does my filled worksheet feed Ch 3 or only Ch 5 Signal? The handoff sentence points one way, the body another. One clarifying line needed.

### THEME I — Two specific numbers / terms that will trip the reader
- **Ch 1 §VI.D** — the same $558K appears under both Constraint Clarity and Measurement Discipline; readers will ask if the two dimensions measure different things.
- **Ch 6 §X** — "Tier 3 (human judgment required)" vs. "Not AI-tier (judgment-bound)" sound identical. Distinguish or merge.
- **Ch 12 §VIII** — "design-validation document, not financial" immediately followed by a table of dollar columns (Cost Before/After/Delta). Resolve the contradiction.
- **Ch 12 §IX** — Sprint-1 delta $446K vs. Sprint-2 $66K: explain *why compounding still works as marginal deltas shrink* (cumulative vs. marginal), not just "that's normal."

## 4. Outlines that should be reworked before a Drafter touches one

**None require a structural rework.** No outline scored "No," and no outline carries logic gaps severe enough to block drafting. However, three carry enough weight that the author should resolve their specific issues *before* the Drafter starts, rather than during:

- **Ch 12 — The Rhythm (10 open questions, the most in the set).** Not broken, but it's the book's payoff chapter and carries the heaviest cluster of unresolved reader questions: the downsizing-vs-growth misread risk in the opener (§II "thirteen to eight"), the "not financial" scorecard contradiction (§VIII), the shrinking-delta math (§IX), the buried payoff (§VII placement), the thin closing (§X), and the Ulrich/COE reference that reads as name-dropping (§IV.B). Decide these before drafting — getting the book's climax wrong is the costliest miss.
- **Ch 11 — Compound.** The headcount-slope payoff (§XI) is trimmed and mis-positioned relative to its importance, and §VI hands the reader a failing grade with no recovery path. These are positioning/proportion decisions the author must make, not the Drafter.
- **Ch 1 — Diagnosis.** The ERP-as-universal-anchor assumption (§IV) genuinely breaks for a real segment of the target reader, and the §V Action Step risks duplicating the §VII Scorecard ("scavenger hunt before the framework"). Resolve the anchor and the Action-Step/Scorecard relationship before drafting, since Ch 1 sets the pattern every later chapter inherits.

Everything else can go to the Drafter with the consolidated answer key in hand.

## 5. Cross-cutting strengths worth preserving

These showed up as strengths repeatedly and should survive drafting untouched:

1. **The syllogism-per-chapter discipline.** Every chapter's premises chain cleanly to its conclusion, and a non-technical CEO can follow the chain without business-school background. This is the single most-praised structural property across all twelve verdicts. Protect it.
2. **Meridian as a single anchored thread.** 27 employees, $7.2M, metal fabrication, quoting bottleneck, Elena Ruiz. Readers locate themselves against Meridian and watch one named operator work the model before they're asked to. Keep Meridian's worked examples adjacent to the concept they illustrate (concept-then-example, not example-much-later). One caveat: introduce Elena before her name is first used (Ch 7b §II uses "Elena-style" before she's introduced).
3. **Fillable artifacts as chapter payoffs** — the 20-question Scorecard, the Sprint Planning Canvas, the Constraint Statement (blank + populated pair), the four-field design-change card, the Compounding Scorecard, the EOS mapping table, Minimum Viable Signal. Converting reading into a working session is what makes this book different from AI-hype titles. Every reader praised these.
4. **Story-first openers.** The marketing-coordinator redesign (Ch 3), the "$24K hire never posted" (Ch 7b), the Argenti banker (Ch 2), the Katie/Jill 30-minute call (Ch 13), the discovery call (Ch 1). Each shows the failure mode in a recognizable scene before any vocabulary arrives. Keep them verbatim where the outlines say "KEEP."
5. **Concrete operational rules a CEO can repeat Monday morning** — "Humans own outcomes, agents own tasks" (Ch 3), the tiebreaker rule (Ch 1), "the Sprint is real when the review is on the calendar" (Ch 4), "if your retrospective produces zero didn't-work items, you didn't run it honestly" (Ch 11), the Bedard 3-agent ceiling (Ch 7), the gate read-aloud test (Ch 5). These are the lines readers said they'd repeat to their teams.
6. **Pro Tips that preempt the CEO's anxiety in the right place** — "the Scorecard isn't a test, it's a map" (Ch 1), "if your Source output doesn't fit on one page, it's not scoped" (Ch 6). Reassurance and guardrails delivered exactly where the reader feels the pull.
7. **The trims and cuts were correct.** Cutting the Sadun citation (Ch 1), HBR 7→1 (Ch 2), the brand/stage-name outro (Ch 11), the two weaker Rhythm illustrations (Ch 12), the standalone "People don't have APIs" section (Ch 6). Every cut sharpened the chapter. Don't reintroduce them.
8. **EOS-native concept adaptations that land naturally** — the HAC's single-accountability rule mapping to the Accountability Chart, Right Seat Evaluation (Sees It / Wants It / Suited for It), the EOS mapping table in Ch 12. The connection is strongest when it's surfaced explicitly (see Theme E) — preserve it and name it.
