**1.1 The Headcount Paradox**

Here is the math you are living right now.

The company is growing. The pipeline is healthy. And your leadership
team is doing what leadership teams do when demand is up: they are
asking to hire. Another account manager. Another ops person. Another
engineer. The assumption is baked in so deep it does not even feel like
an assumption. To grow means to add people.

You bought some AI subscriptions. Maybe ran a pilot or two. The intent
was right. And here is what makes this a paradox: you are willing to
invest more. The budget is there. But you do not know where to aim. You
do not know what is possible, so you do not know what to buy, what to
build, or what return to expect. The headcount curve has not bent, not
because AI failed you, but because you have not been able to connect AI
spend to operating change.

Meanwhile your integrator or COO is on the calendar asking to approve
the next two roles. The numbers in the request are reasonable. The work
is real, the team is stretched, the demand is there. You sign off,
because the alternative is throttling growth. But you sign off knowing
the AI investment was supposed to make at least one of those
requisitions unnecessary, and you cannot point to the reason it did not.

This is the headcount paradox. AI was supposed to change the math of
scaling. It has not. Revenue and headcount are still moving together.
The cost of one keeps climbing. And the investment made to break the
link is sitting on the books producing nothing structural.

**1.2 A Word About Jobs**

Before we go any further, we want to be direct about something.

Every conversation about AI eventually arrives at the same fear. What
about the jobs? Is AI going to eliminate them? Are the people who build
these systems genuinely helping organizations, or are they building the
machinery of their own workforce\'s obsolescence? We hear this question
in every room, from CEOs who are worried about their teams and from team
members who are worried about themselves. We want to answer it plainly,
because we think the fear is being amplified far beyond what the
evidence supports, and because the organizations making the best
decisions about AI are the ones that have moved past the fear and into
the actual design work.

We do not believe AI is going to take over everyone\'s jobs. Full stop.

What we believe, and what the organizations we have worked with most
closely are demonstrating, is something quite different. AI is capable
of doing the task-level work that should never have been the primary
assignment of a capable human being in the first place. The data entry.
The report generation. The routing of standard inquiries to the right
queue. The assembly of information from multiple systems into a summary
that a human then acts on. That work is real, it is necessary, and it
has been consuming enormous amounts of human capacity in every
organization we have ever advised. When it moves to an agent team, the
humans who were doing it are not replaced. They are freed.

Freed for what? For the work that actually requires a human. The
judgment calls. The relationship management. The strategic thinking. The
mentorship. The situations where context, ethics, and accountability
cannot be delegated to a system. The work that, if you ask most people
who are doing primarily task-level jobs what they wish they could spend
more time on, is exactly what they will describe. They are not trying to
protect their right to do data entry. They are doing data entry because
nobody ever designed an alternative.

There is also a demographic reality that makes this moment different
from every previous technology transition. We are not in a period of
population growth. The workforce that will be available to organizations
over the next two decades is not going to be dramatically larger than
the one available today. In many sectors and geographies, it will be
smaller. The organizations that are planning to grow their revenue by
growing their headcount in proportion are planning against a labor
supply that may not exist. The organizations that are learning to grow
their revenue by growing the capability of the workforce they have,
through the deliberate design of synthetic teams that amplify human
capacity, are planning for the actual future.

This is why we believe the introduction of Co-Intelligent Co-operation
is not just an operational improvement opportunity. It is a strategic
necessity. Organizations that figure out how to design the work so that
humans and agents each contribute what they are best suited to
contribute will be able to grow without depending on a headcount
expansion that the demographics may not support. Organizations that do
not will face a structural ceiling on growth that technology cannot
solve once the talent supply has tightened.

The practical consequence of this belief is visible in every sprint we
run. When an agent team takes the task-level work in a workflow, the
human in that role does not leave. They rise. Elena at Meridian did not
lose her job when the Quoting Agent took fifteen hours of her week. She
got those fifteen hours to apply to the strategic operations work that
had been on her backlog for two years. Her job did not get smaller. It
got more worth showing up for. Her retention is not at risk because of
the sprint. Her retention is more secure because of it. People stay in
organizations where their work is meaningful and their judgment is
valued. The sprint program, properly designed and managed, moves
organizations in exactly that direction.

We will return to this point in Chapter 11, where we discuss managing
the synthetic workforce in depth. For now, the principle to carry
through the rest of this book is this: the goal of every sprint is not
to eliminate work. It is to elevate the humans doing it.

**1.3 Why AI Investments Stall**

The reflexive move when AI does not produce the expected return is to
blame the tools: assume the technology was oversold or that the next
version will finally deliver. The tools are more capable than most
operators realize. The problem is not their capability. It is that there
is no defined relationship between the AI system and the work it is
supposed to do. No designed workflow. No clear accountability. No
governance. The tool is ambient. It helps individuals occasionally. It
changes nothing structurally. And structural is the only kind of change
that fixes the math.

The reason is the operating model. The company was designed,
deliberately or by drift, to be run by humans alone. You then introduced
a second kind of worker: synthetic, capable, available. And dropped it
next to the structure without changing the structure. The new worker has
no seat, no accountability, no defined handoff. So it produces nothing
the organization can compound on.

Every major transformation initiative of the last thirty years has
failed at the same point. The ERP implementation nobody used. The
post-merger integration that destroyed more value than it created. The
lean rollout that reverted within eighteen months. In every case the
same root cause: the capability arrived and the structure never changed
to receive it. AI is the latest version of this failure. The technology
is different. The structural error is identical.

If you walked into any function in your company today and asked three
questions: what is this function accountable for, what is its scorecard,
and who reviews the output on what cadence, you would get clean answers
for the humans and blank stares for the AI. That is the diagnosis in one
move. The AI in your company has no seat on the chart. It has no number
it is hitting. It has no review rhythm. You would never run a function
that way if it were staffed by people. You are running it that way
because you have not yet decided that AI is staff.

**1.4 The Wrong Question**

There is one specific trap every organization falls into at the start of
its AI effort: it looks exactly like the right approach.

The first question most teams ask is task-oriented: which of our
existing tasks could AI help with? It sounds reasonable. It sounds
practical. And it is not wrong; it gets you some of the way there. You
will find prompt tips, a few useful shortcuts, some real time saved. But
task-orientation is woefully insufficient. It adds AI on top of the
existing workflow. The existing workflow was designed for humans. So the
gains stay small, the wins stay scattered, and nothing changes
structurally.

The question that unlocks real value is goal-oriented: what outcomes are
we accountable for, and how should the human and AI workforce be
designed to deliver them? That question does not start with the tools.
It starts with outcomes, and then with accountability for those
outcomes. It produces a deliberate design: who owns what, what the human
reviews, what the agent team executes, where the handoff lives, how the
result gets measured.

Start with the outcome the organization is accountable for. Design the
work backward from that outcome. Who owns what. What requires judgment.
What can be systematized. Where the handoffs live. That discipline,
outcome first, design second, tool or role third, is what makes the
difference between an initiative that compounds and one that disappears.
AI did not invent this discipline. It made the cost of skipping it
catastrophically visible.

**1.5 Where You Actually Stand**

Before you move forward, you need to know where you actually stand right
now.

The AI Readiness Scorecard [\[insert website link\]]{.mark} covers five
dimensions. For each one, score yourself Red, Yellow, or Green. Do this
with whoever would be in the room if you were deciding whether to invest
in AI. Not just you: the team. And be honest. The scorecard is not a
test. It is a compass.

The five dimensions come from two directions. The technical systems
experience behind this book identified Information Readiness and
Workflow Visibility as the preconditions that determine whether an AI
agent can actually do the work. The human capital experience identified
Constraint Clarity, Decision Rights, and Measurement Discipline as the
preconditions that determine whether the company will accept and hold
the change once the technology is deployed. A company can be technically
ready and organizationally unready. Both halves have to be green for a
sprint to succeed.

**1.5.1 Constraint Clarity**

Can your leadership team name the ONE operational constraint AI should
solve? The degree of agreement in the room tells you more than any
single answer. Agreement means you have a starting point. Disagreement
means the constraint is not the workflow: it is the leadership team\'s
alignment on what matters most.

**1.5.2 Information Readiness**

Is the knowledge your team runs on documented and accessible, or trapped
in people\'s heads? Here is the test: if your best operations person
quit tomorrow, how long before the replacement could run the workflow
without calling them? If the answer is months, you are Red regardless of
what is in your wiki.

**1.5.3 Workflow Visibility**

Can you draw the workflow where the constraint lives, including who does
what and where handoffs break? The gap between Yellow and Green here is
not documentation: it is agreement. A workflow that one person can
describe is a story. A workflow the team has validated is a map. Agent
teams need the map.

**1.5.4 Decision Rights**

Does your team know who decides what AI handles versus what stays with
humans? Decision rights are not about AI capability. They are about
organizational trust. The question is not \'Can AI do this?\' It is \'Do
we trust the output enough to act on it without a human reviewing it?\'
That is a leadership decision, not a technology decision.

**1.5.5 Measurement Discipline**

Can you put a dollar number on what the constraint costs per quarter? If
you cannot measure what the constraint costs today, you cannot measure
whether AI made it better tomorrow. Measurement discipline is the
difference between an AI initiative and an AI experiment. Experiments
are optional. Initiatives have ROI targets.

**2. The Co-Operating Model**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

We were in a management meeting, going through the accountability chart.
There was a team member, a marketing coordinator, who was good at
organization. She could manage processes, track content calendars,
coordinate subcontractors. But she was not revenue-positive and had not
been for months. She was spending most of her time on content
production: editing videos, formatting posts, work that took hours and
still was not shipping fast enough.

We looked at the accountability chart and asked the real question: what
if we stopped designing this seat for a human doing linear production
work, and started designing it for a human managing agent-driven
workstreams? What if the coordinator\'s job was not to create the
content but to direct the agents creating it, manage the quality, and
make sure it shipped?

That conversation changed the shape of the role. It also changed how we
thought about every other role on the chart. What happened in that room
is what organizational designers call work deconstruction: the explicit
process of separating tasks by the type of intelligence required to
perform them. Tasks that require judgment, taste, context, and
accountability stay with humans. Tasks that follow rules, patterns, and
defined outputs can be systematized. Most organizations have never done
this work explicitly. They have grown their role definitions
organically, layering tasks onto people until the person becomes the
system. When the person leaves, the system collapses. When AI arrives,
it has no designed relationship to the work because the work was never
designed at all. It was just assigned.

That is an operating model failure. And the Co-Operating Model is the
answer to it.

**2.1 What a Co-Intelligent Company Is**

Three terms in this chapter are doing specific work and they are easy to
blur together.

The Co-Intelligent Company is the identity layer: what a company becomes
when humans and AI work as one system, not two parallel tracks. The bar
is higher than \'uses AI tools\' or \'has an AI strategy.\' From an the
design lens perspective, a Co-Intelligent Company is one where the
accountability chart has been explicitly extended to include AI agent
teams alongside humans, where every AI-handled function has a named
human supervisor, a defined scope of work, and a review cadence. Most
companies using AI today have not crossed that bar.

Co-Intelligent Co-operation is the relational frame: the activity of
humans and AI working the same problems together, inside the same loop,
against the same outcome. Not relegated to a sprint or a project. It is
what the company does, continuously, as its default way of working.

The Co-Operating Model is the system frame: the structure that makes
Co-Intelligent Co-operation possible. It is the explicit design of who
brings what intelligence to the work and who owns which operating
responsibility inside it.

The coordinator in the opening story was inside a company that uses AI.
But the company did not yet have a Co-Operating Model. The difference
was not the tools. It was the operating model that had not been built
yet.

**2.2 What AI Actually Is**

Most executives have met one kind of AI: a chat window. You type
something, it responds. You close the tab. Those tools are helping
people do their jobs slightly faster, and that is real. But you hit a
ceiling quickly. The gains flatten because the shape of the work has not
changed.

A chatbot is an interface. You ask a question, you get an answer, the
interaction ends. An agent is different. An agent is an AI system that
holds a goal, takes a sequence of steps toward it, evaluates its own
output, and adjusts, without you re-prompting it between steps. It does
not wait. It works. The distinction between chatbot and agent is the
distinction between a tool that answers questions and a workforce that
does work.

The technology underneath both is the same: large language models
trained on massive amounts of text to recognize and generate language at
scale. What separates a chatbot from an agent is the architecture around
the model: how it is instructed, what tools it has access to, how it
evaluates its own output, and how it hands work back to a human or to
another agent.

One more concept matters for the operating model: organizational memory.
Most AI tools, out of the box, have no knowledge of your company. There
are technologies that fix this. They connect an agent to your CRM, your
transcripts, your SOPs, so it can search and reason against your own
documents instead of just general training. The result is a system that
reasons against your actual context. An agent that knows your pricing
rules, your client history, your standard escalation paths, not because
you paste that context in every time, but because the connection was
designed in.

The organizational knowledge vulnerability this solves is not
hypothetical. When a developer who had built systems for years left a
software company, the knowledge left with him. No system, no process, no
designed mechanism to make sure the investment made in his learning
stayed inside the business when he did not. That issue, \'we do not own
our knowledge in our own business,\' surfaces constantly. An
organization with a well-built memory layer does not have that exposure.
The knowledge stays because it was designed to stay.

**2.3 Why the Co Is Load-Bearing**

The last decade of enterprise software pitched a graft: a productivity
layer bolted on top of the existing org. The metaphor was addition. You
had a worker; now the worker has a tool. The shape of the work, the
shape of the org, the shape of the accountabilities, none of that
changed much.

This pattern repeated in every major organizational transformation of
the last thirty years. When a global company completed a major
acquisition, the first instinct from every function was to graft new
responsibilities onto existing roles. An existing business partner now
covered three additional countries. An existing procurement lead managed
two additional supplier networks. The result was predictable: grafted
responsibilities performed inconsistently, people stretched past
capacity, and within eighteen months the organization had to restructure
anyway, this time by actually redesigning the work rather than adding it
to existing roles. The redesign produced a more capable organization
with lower total headcount than the graft had required. The graft
produced cost. The redesign produced leverage.

Co-Intelligence is different. The human and the agent are both inside
the loop. The work itself gets reshaped around the fact that two kinds
of intelligence are now executing it. The structure is built to evolve,
because the agent gets smarter every quarter, the human\'s task space
shifts as a result, and the design has to keep pace. The Co is what
gives the company the mechanism to keep improving.

**2.4 What Each One Brings**

The agent is idle until someone tells it what to do. It has no
curiosity. It has no agenda. It does not know what quarter you are in,
what your biggest customer is threatening, or what good looks like for
your company this year. It knows none of that until you transmit it.

Humans carry a world model: the accumulated understanding built
constantly from every conversation, every mistake, every relationship,
every quarter lived inside the business. That model tells us what
matters, what a decision costs, what a number means when it lands inside
the context of everything else we know. We use it so automatically we
forget it is there. Agents do not have one. They have pattern
recognition at a scale no human can match, and memory that does not
degrade. But they have no ground truth about your situation and no felt
sense of what is at stake.

Think about the worst manager you ever had. The one who handed you a
task with no background, no success criteria, no sense of what they
actually needed or why it mattered. You did your best. The work landed
badly. The failure was not yours. It was the manager\'s failure to
transmit. We do the same thing to agents. Constantly.

The human\'s job in the Co-Operating Model is to be the transmitter of a
world model and a desired outcome. The agent is the executor that can
only work from what was transmitted. Get that transmission right and the
agent produces work that is hard to distinguish from what a skilled
person would produce. Get it wrong and you get the equivalent of that
badly briefed employee: technically busy, contextually adrift.

**2.5 What Each One Owns**

Ownership is where most companies stall, and it is the variable that
most determines whether the work compounds.

The way we use ownership here is specific. It does not mean who is aware
of a task or who is copied on the outcome. It means who is accountable
for the decision, the quality, and the next move if something goes
wrong. One person, or one agent, per responsibility. Not shared. A
single owner who picks up the work and does not put it down until the
handoff is complete. In the Co-Operating Model, a human always holds
ultimate ownership. They might delegate to an agent or a team of agents,
but organizations hold humans responsible for outcomes, even in fully
automated processes.

Here is what that looks like in practice. Our marketing lead manages
content and video production for Compound. Once she decides what we are
publishing, which topic, which angle, which channel, the agent takes the
brief and produces the first draft. She does not write the first
sentence. She reads the output, marks what is right and what is wrong,
and the agent revises. The draft never leaves her without her judgment
on it. She owns the outcome. The agent owns the output that precedes it.

The Right Seat Evaluation we apply to every Headcount Accountability
Chart (HAC) supervisor assignment asks three questions: Does this person
See It? Can they evaluate whether the agent\'s output is correct, or
will they simply approve whatever comes back because they cannot tell
the difference? Do they Want It? Are they genuinely accountable for the
outcome, or do they view the agent team as someone else\'s
responsibility with their name attached? Are they Suited for It? Do they
have the judgment, the context, and the authority to make the calls the
agent cannot make and to redirect the agent when it drifts? A supervisor
who fails any of these tests is not a supervisor. They are a liability.

The human side is almost entirely verbs of judgment and direction:
decides, directs, prices, owns, coaches. The agent side is almost
entirely verbs of execution and capture: drafts, recommends, documents,
monitors, executes. The human is not in the workflow as a faster
operator. The human is in the workflow as the operator of the operators.
That is the shift that makes the math work.

**2.6 The End of Linear Work**

Our marketing lead manages content and video production for Compound.
Before we applied the model, she was the doer: writing the copy, editing
the video, building the assets, one task at a time. Linear work.

After, we stopped and looked at what was actually happening on her
screen. Claude was working on visual assets, generating layout options,
iterating on brand-consistent graphics. Another AI assistant was
drafting copy: headlines, body text, distribution notes. NotebookLM was
pulling and synthesizing source material, scanning transcripts,
surfacing themes. HeyGen was handling video, producing clips from
scripts, syncing audio, generating cuts. Four agent teams. Four
workstreams. All pointed at the same output.

Her job did not get smaller. It got higher. She was not editing a video.
She was not writing a caption. She was deciding whether the outputs were
right. She was steering.

That shift has a structural name: parallel workstreams. Multiple
agent-driven work tracks running simultaneously under one human\'s
direction. Linear work, finish this before starting that, made sense
when all the workers were human. A person can only work one task at a
time. The constraint was biological. Agents do not have that constraint.
The work that used to move in a line now moves in parallel, and the
wall-clock time collapses.

The same principle operates at the human level. Think about any
executive who runs a business unit and chairs a board committee and
mentors a team simultaneously without any of the three consuming the
others. That is not exceptional capacity. It is parallel design: each
responsibility has its own cadence, its own inputs, its own defined
window in the week. They do not compete for time because they were not
built to share it. The discipline that makes that work for a human is
exactly the discipline that makes it work for an agent team: define the
scope, design the rhythm, run them in parallel. The constraint is not
bandwidth. It is always design.

The compounding math is direct. Parallelism reduces calendar time for
every sprint. Reduced sprint time means more sprints per quarter. More
sprints means more learning cycles, more infrastructure built, more
returns compounding. A Co-Operating Model designed for parallel
workstreams does not just produce output faster. It compounds faster.

**2.7 The Equation**

Everything in this chapter reduces to one line.

Co-Intelligence + Rhythm = Compound.

The left side holds two halves that are useless without each other.
Co-Intelligence is the system half: the Co-Operating Model, the designed
structure where human and agent intelligence do their respective work
against the same problems. Rhythm is the cadence half: running the
system on a quarterly beat, sprint by sprint, against your real
operational constraints.

Together they produce the right side: Compound, used as a verb, in the
precise sense your CFO means it. Each sprint produces a return. The
infrastructure built in that sprint reduces the cost of the next sprint.
The design improvements made between sprints accelerate every sprint
that follows. The company compounds on its AI investment the same way a
balance sheet compounds on retained earnings. The gains start small.
They do not stay small.

A company with the model but no rhythm has a slide deck. A company with
rhythm but no model has activity. Both halves, on the same beat, against
real constraints: that produces the outcome.

**3. The Framework**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

She is on the screen and we can tell from the way she is sitting that
she has already made up her mind. We have been talking for maybe twenty
minutes, her company, her bottlenecks, what she is hoping AI can fix,
and then she leans forward and says it: \'Can we just build it? I just
need this thing built.\'

We know exactly what she means. She has seen demos. She has read the
case studies. She has a clear picture in her head of the tool that is
going to solve her problem, and she wants to stop talking about it and
start building it. That is not impatience. That is the instinct of
someone who has run a business. When you know what you want, you build
it.

But we have also watched what happens when you start there. The build
does not go well. The requirements shift two weeks in because nobody
mapped what the work actually required. Edge cases surface that nobody
anticipated because nobody understood the process well enough to
anticipate them. The tool gets deployed eventually, in some version of
what was originally scoped, and then the team does not use it because it
does not fit how the work actually moves.

We said: let us start at Signal. She said: okay. We did. What she found
there surprised her. The constraint she had been carrying in her head
for six months was not the real constraint. It was a symptom of
something two steps upstream. If we had built what she came in asking
for, we would have solved the wrong problem.

That is the conversation this chapter exists to prevent you from having
with yourself.

The principle behind this is not new. It is the foundational discipline
of this field practice: diagnose before you prescribe. Understand the
system before you change it. Map the work before you redesign it. Every
major implementation failure either of us has been part of shared the
same root cause. The solution was built before the problem was
understood. The diagnosis was skipped because it felt slow and abstract
and like it did not produce visible output. So everyone moved to what
felt like progress, which was building, and discovered twelve months
later they had built the wrong thing at great expense.

Signal exists to prevent that. It is the discipline of refusing to build
until the problem has been precisely named and its cost has been
quantified.

**3.1 The Order Is the Argument**

Most AI initiatives skip the diagnosis and start at Build. A tool gets
chosen, a budget gets spent, a workflow gets launched, and around month
nine the CEO is sitting in a meeting trying to explain why the AI line
item produced no operational result. The cause is not the tool. The
cause is the order.

Building before diagnosing is the same mistake as framing a house before
pouring the foundation. The walls go up. The roof goes on. It looks like
progress right up until the moment the structure has to carry weight.
Construction has rules about the order of operations because the order
is load-bearing. So does the Compound Sprint.

The Framework exists because there is a correct sequence for installing
co-intelligence into a company, and that sequence is the entire reason
the result compounds instead of evaporating. Skip the first two steps
and you are buying a tool. Run all six in order and you are changing how
the work is owned.

**3.2 Four Words, Defined Once**

This book uses four words constantly and they are easy to conflate. Each
names a different thing.

> Framework: the whole system. When we say \'the Framework\' or \'the
> Compound Framework,\' we mean everything described in this book: the
> order, the stages, the cadence, the unit of work, and the acceleration
> layers that make each cycle faster.
>
> Sequence: the ordered stages inside the Framework. Signal, Source,
> Design, Build, Deliver, Compound. Always all six. Always in that
> order, because the order is load-bearing. Each stage produces
> something the next stage requires.
>
> Sprint: one pass through the Sequence on one validated constraint.
> Duration is set in Design. A Sprint can close in days, run for weeks,
> or stretch across a quarter, depending on what the constraint
> requires. You do not implement the Framework. You run a Sprint, and
> then you run another one.
>
> Rhythm: the cadence. Running the Framework over and over. Quarterly.
> The Rhythm is what turns a single Sprint into a compounding business
> outcome, the same way a weekly L10 is what turns a quarterly Rock into
> a company that actually executes.

**3.3 The Six Stages, at a Glance**

The Sequence is six stages. Each gets its own chapter. Here is the role
of each one.

> 1\. Signal: you find the one operational constraint worth solving
> next, and quantify what it costs.
>
> 2\. Source: you map what the work actually requires. The knowledge,
> the data, the judgment, the context.
>
> 3\. Design: you decide who does what across the Human+AI workforce for
> this specific constraint.
>
> 4\. Build: you produce the spec and deploy the workflow into the
> system your team already uses.
>
> 5\. Deliver: you ship it inside the org, train the people whose
> handoffs change, and measure the result against what Signal said it
> cost.
>
> 6\. Compound: you capture the learning, update the infrastructure the
> next Sprint will use, and rank what comes next.

Six stages. One Sprint. Then again.

**3.4 Diagnose, Then Execute and Compound**

The Sequence splits into two halves. The first half is Diagnose: Signal
and Source. The second half is Execute and Compound: Design, Build,
Deliver, Compound.

Diagnose is the work most companies skip. They skip it because there is
no structured way to approach it. The inputs are abstract and without a
framework, people default to what is concrete: building. In a physical
process, burned toast is obvious. In an information process, a
misclassified taxonomy in a knowledge base causing an agent to query the
wrong data and produce wrong answers is invisible until the damage is
done. The Sequence exists to make the abstract concrete. Diagnose gives
you the structure to see what you would otherwise skip past, and
everything downstream depends on what it finds.

A Sprint that gets stuck in Build almost never has a Build problem. It
has a Diagnose problem that surfaced late. Going back to Signal is not
failure. It is the Framework doing its job, refusing to let undisclosed
assumptions hide inside execution.

**3.5 The Sprint Planning Canvas**

Before you run your first Sprint, you sketch it. The Sprint Planning
Canvas is a one-page artifact that names the constraint, maps your
first-pass answers to each stage, identifies the team, and locks the
review date. It is intentionally incomplete. The point is to start with
a compass, not a blank page.

The canvas has five parts.

> 1\. Name the Sprint. Write the constraint in one sentence. Name the
> leadership sponsor. Pick the quarter.
>
> 2\. Map the Six Stages. One honest first guess per stage. You do not
> need perfect answers. You need something on paper so the chapters that
> follow have something to sharpen.
>
> 3\. Identify Your Team. A Sprint is not a committee. You need three to
> five people: a Human Orchestrator, the person who runs the Sprint
> day-to-day, plus the two or three people closest to the constraint
> workflow.
>
> 4\. Set the Rhythm. Name the start date, the quarterly operating
> session where you will review the outcome, and who will be in that
> review. Book the review before you start Signal.
>
> 5\. Pre-Flight Checklist. Before starting Signal, confirm five things:
> leadership team agrees this constraint is worth a Sprint, one person
> is named as Human Orchestrator, the team has access to the data and
> people they will need for Source, there is a realistic expectation
> that a build can happen this quarter, and the quarterly review is on
> the calendar.

**3.5.1 What a Populated Canvas Looks Like**

Meridian Manufacturing. \$7.2M revenue, twenty-seven employees, custom
metal fabrication. Mark Ellison is the CEO. Elena Ruiz is the VP of
Operations and the only person who can produce a complete quote. Quotes
take three to five business days. Competitors turn them in twenty-four
to forty-eight hours. Meridian is losing bids not on price but on speed.

Constraint: Elena Ruiz is the sole quoting bottleneck, costing an
estimated \$558K per year in delayed and lost revenue. Leadership
sponsor: Mark Ellison. Human Orchestrator: Elena Ruiz. Quarter: Q3.
Team: Elena (domain owner), Dave Kowalski (fabrication expertise), Ty
Banfield (sales lead). Quarterly review scheduled before Signal begins.

That canvas took thirty minutes to fill. It is wrong in at least two
places, and the team knows which ones. Signal will correct them. The
point was never to be right on the first pass. The point was to stop
staring at a blank page.

**3.6 You Learn This on a Sprint**

You do not learn the Framework by reading about it. You learn it by
running one Sprint on one real constraint in your own company. That is
the only teaching unit that works, because the Framework is not a model
of how AI implementation could go. It is the structure of how the work
moves when it moves correctly.

The Sprint is bounded by scope, not by a fixed calendar. The narrowness
is the discipline that prevents the failure mode every operator has
seen: an AI project becoming a platform project, a platform project
becoming a reorg, and eighteen months later nothing has shipped. One
constraint, one documented outcome. That is the bar.

The Rhythm is what turns that one Sprint into a compounding business.
Quarterly, you run another one. The Hybrid Accountability Chart gains
new entries. The knowledge maps get richer. The team\'s design judgment
improves. The Sprints themselves get cheaper and faster, because the
infrastructure each Sprint leaves behind accelerates the next one.

**3.7 Begin at Signal**

Every Sprint begins in the same place. Before Source can map anything,
before Design can allocate anything, before Build can specify anything,
Signal has to name the one constraint this Sprint will solve. The
discipline of refusing to move forward until that constraint is named,
quantified, and validated is the single most important habit the
Framework teaches.

**4. Signal**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The sections that follow are where Signal becomes operational. The the
operating discipline discipline behind Signal does not disappear when
the process begins. It intensifies. Every step of the Signal process is
a design decision about how the organization will name, own, and commit
to the problem it is about to spend a quarter solving. The facilitation
skill required to run Signal well is not technical. It is
organizational: holding a leadership team to the discipline of one
constraint, preventing the political drift toward vague commitments, and
refusing to call Signal complete until the number is real and the
commitment is public.

**4.5 How to Run Signal Yourself**

Signal is a facilitated working session. The first time you run it, plan
for two to three hours with your full leadership team. Not a subset. Not
the CEO and the integrator alone. The people who will be affected by the
sprint\'s outcomes need to be in the room where the constraint is named,
because a constraint statement that the broader leadership team did not
participate in naming is a constraint statement that will face
resistance when the Design decisions require their cooperation.

This is one of the most consistent findings from the systematic approach
practice. When a leadership team is presented with a change decision
rather than invited into the diagnostic process, the change faces
structural resistance that has nothing to do with the quality of the
decision. The people who were not in the room do not feel accountable
for an outcome they did not choose. Signal solves this upstream: the
constraint is not delivered to the team, it emerges from the team. Every
person in the room carries some ownership of the statement they helped
produce.

The facilitator\'s job in Signal is not to find the constraint. It is to
create the conditions under which the team finds it together and commits
to it publicly. That is a human leadership skill, not an AI capability,
and it is the reason Signal is the stage where the people leader is most
critical.

**4.5.1 Step 1: Surface the Candidates**

Ask the leadership team four questions. Run them in sequence without
filtering. You are building a candidate pool, not making decisions. The
decisions come later, and they are better when they are made against a
full field of candidates rather than the two or three that were loudest
in last week\'s L10.

> 1\. What shows up on every issues list but never gets resolved? Not
> the issue that was raised once. The issue that has been raised in
> every quarterly conversation for two years and has never been closed.
> That pattern is diagnostic: the issue persists not because nobody
> cares but because the structural cause has never been addressed.
>
> 2\. What does a new person always find confusing in their first ninety
> days? New-hire confusion is one of the most underused organizational
> design diagnostics available. A person who is new to the organization
> sees the gaps that insiders have learned to work around. Their
> confusion is not a training problem. It is a design signal.
>
> 3\. What process would we never let a customer watch? Every
> organization has a process that works well enough to produce an
> acceptable output and badly enough that transparency about how it
> produces that output would be embarrassing. Those processes are the
> highest-cost constraints because the cost of the broken design is
> being absorbed every day across every person who touches the workflow.
>
> 4\. What conversation keeps coming up in meetings but never gets
> resolved? Recurring conversations without resolution are structural:
> the conversation recurs because the structure that would resolve it
> has never been built. Naming those conversations as constraint
> candidates moves them out of the interpersonal realm and into the this
> kind of work realm, where they can actually be addressed.

List every answer. Do not filter. Do not qualify. Do not explain why
something cannot be a sprint candidate. Every answer goes on the board.
Filtering happens in Step 2.

**4.5.2 Step 2: Trace Symptoms to Constraints**

The candidate pool almost always contains more symptoms than
constraints. This is normal. Leadership teams are trained to name
problems in terms of their operational experience: things feel slow,
things feel unclear, things feel understaffed. Those are all real
experiences. None of them is a constraint statement.

For each candidate, run the Is/Is Not test. Draw two columns. The left
column is \'where and when this problem IS present.\' The right column
is \'where and when it is NOT present.\' The pattern that emerges is
almost always more revealing than any single answer in the left column.
If the quoting problem exists in the enterprise accounts but not in the
standard accounts, the constraint lives in the complexity of the
enterprise quoting workflow, not in quoting capacity broadly. That
specificity is what transforms a symptom into a constraint.

The the structural work framing behind the Is/Is Not test is worth
naming explicitly. Every organizational problem has a boundary: a set of
conditions under which it appears and a set under which it does not.
Finding that boundary is the work of constraint identification. The
Is/Is Not test is a structured way to find the boundary without letting
the team collapse into abstract theorizing about why things happen. It
keeps the conversation grounded in observable patterns.

**4.5.3 Step 3: Run the Five Constraint Questions**

For each candidate that survived the Is/Is Not test, run five questions.
These are not rhetorical. Each question produces a specific output that
either advances the candidate toward a sprint commitment or reveals that
more diagnostic work is needed before the candidate is sprint-ready.

> 1\. Is this within the organization\'s control to change? Constraints
> that depend on market conditions, regulatory decisions, or factors
> outside the organization\'s operating authority are not sprint
> constraints. Not because they are unimportant, but because a sprint
> changes what the organization does, not what the market does. A sprint
> on \'we cannot find qualified people to hire\' is solving the wrong
> problem. A sprint on \'our recruiting workflow is missing the
> candidates who exist\' is a sprint the organization can run and win.
>
> 2\. What does it cost per year? Write a number. This question is the
> most consistently resisted question in the Signal process, and the
> resistance is always the same: \'We do not have exact data on that.\'
> That answer is accepted exactly once. The second time it appears, it
> is diagnosed as avoidance. Every constraint has a cost. Some costs are
> in dollars. Some are in hours. Some are in margin. Some are in lost
> revenue. All of them can be estimated. A team that genuinely cannot
> estimate the cost of a problem it has been living with for two years
> is a team that has not been managing to outcomes. Signal forces that
> conversation, which is part of why it matters.
>
> 3\. How long has it existed? Constraints that have been present for
> more than a year are structural, not situational. That distinction
> matters for the sprint design. A situational problem can often be
> resolved with a process improvement or a personnel decision. A
> structural problem has persisted despite multiple attempts at
> resolution because the structure underneath it was never redesigned.
> Structural constraints are exactly what the Compound Sprint was built
> to address.
>
> 4\. Is there a single person who could be the Human Orchestrator? This
> question is not about the sprint execution yet. It is a diagnostic: if
> the team cannot name one person who knows the constraint workflow well
> enough to own a sprint against it, the constraint may be too diffuse,
> or the knowledge around it may be too distributed, to sprint on
> effectively. The right answer does not have to be a senior leader. It
> is the person who knows the work well enough to evaluate whether an
> agent\'s output on that work is correct.
>
> 5\. Does the leadership team agree this is the most valuable
> constraint to solve next? This is the commitment question, and it is
> the question most likely to surface political dynamics that the
> earlier steps allowed the team to sidestep. Committing to one
> constraint for an entire quarter means not sprinting on the other
> candidates. That commitment costs something. The team that cannot make
> it has not finished Signal. The constraint is not locked until
> everyone in the room can say yes to this question.

**4.5.4 Step 4: Pick One. Lock It.**

From the surviving candidates, pick one. The organizational design
discipline behind this step is worth understanding before experiencing
the resistance it produces, because the resistance is predictable and it
is worth knowing in advance that the resistance is not evidence that the
step is wrong.

Leadership teams resist picking one constraint for the same reason
organizations resist defining clear accountability: picking one means
letting the others wait. It means someone\'s problem is not the
Sprint\'s problem this quarter. It means the CEO of the sales function
cannot point to the sprint as evidence that his function\'s constraint
is being addressed. These are real organizational dynamics, and they
will appear in the Signal session as reasons why the team should sprint
on two constraints simultaneously, or why this particular constraint
should be broken into sub-constraints that span multiple functions.

The discipline holds anyway. One constraint. One Sprint. The reason is
not arbitrary. It is structural. A sprint produces a measurable result
against a named baseline. Two sprints running simultaneously produce two
partial results against two baselines, neither of which is complete, and
the leadership team cannot point to either as definitive evidence that
the methodology works. The first sprint\'s job, in addition to solving a
constraint, is to prove to the organization that the Sprint model
produces a result. That proof requires a clean line from the constraint
cost identified in Signal to the cost reduction measured in Deliver. Two
constraints contaminate that line.

Write the constraint statement in one sentence using the template. Share
it with the room. Ask each person to confirm their agreement. If anyone
hesitates, that hesitation is Signal work that is not yet done. Do not
move to Source until the constraint is genuinely locked, not just
declared locked.

**4.5.5 Step 5: Run the Failure Mode Checklist**

Before calling Signal complete, the facilitator runs five failure mode
checks. These are not optional and they are not sequential: all five
must pass before Source begins.

> 1\. The constraint is a symptom, not a root cause. Run the Five Whys
> one more time on the locked constraint statement. If the fifth Why
> produces a different, more specific structural cause than the
> statement currently names, rewrite the statement. This is not uncommon
> on the first pass. Most teams land on a constraint that is
> approximately right but slightly upstream of the real structural
> issue. The Five Whys, run a second time after the political pressure
> of picking one has been resolved, often produces a sharper statement.
>
> 2\. The constraint is too broad to sprint on. If solving the
> constraint as stated would require more than one quarter of focused
> effort from the team, scope it down to the most valuable
> sub-constraint within it. A sprint is a bounded unit of change.
> Bounded means it has an end condition that can be tested in Deliver. A
> constraint that cannot be meaningfully addressed in one quarter is a
> program, not a sprint. Find the sub-constraint that is both the
> highest-value and the most sprint-sized element of the broader
> problem.
>
> 3\. The cost estimate is a guess with no data behind it. If the team
> produced a number in Step 3 but that number was not validated against
> any actual operational data, the team needs to commit to validating it
> as the first act of Source. Unvalidated cost estimates create two
> problems: they may lead the team to sprint on a constraint that,
> properly quantified, is not worth the sprint investment, and they
> leave the Deliver measurement without a credible baseline.
>
> 4\. The leadership sponsor is not engaged. A sprint without a
> committed sponsor stalls at the Design Gate. The Design Gate requires
> accountability decisions, including decisions about which tasks the
> organization will stop doing manually and which it will transfer to an
> agent team. Those decisions require leadership authority. If the
> person with that authority is not genuinely committed to the sprint,
> the Design decisions will not hold, and the sprint will be undone by
> whoever has the authority to reassign accountability back to the
> humans who were released from those tasks by the design.
>
> 5\. The Human Orchestrator has not been confirmed. The name must be in
> the Sprint Planning Canvas before Source begins. An unconfirmed Human
> Orchestrator produces a Source process that has no one driving it to
> completion, a Design process that has no one owning the accountability
> decisions, and a Build that has no one to hand off to when the
> technical work is done.

**4.6 Where the Constraint Comes From Inside an EOS or Scaling Up Shop**

If your company runs on EOS or Scaling Up, you already have Signal
infrastructure. Your Issues list is a constraint backlog that updates
every week. Your Rocks are quarterly commitments to solving the
highest-priority issues. Your L10 is the accountability rhythm that
keeps those commitments current.

Signal adds one layer that EOS does not build in by default:
quantification. EOS identifies and ranks issues by priority. Signal
requires you to put a dollar number on the constraint before committing
a sprint to it. That number transforms the issue from a qualitative
priority into an investment thesis. The leadership team is not just
committing to solving a problem. It is committing to a return on the
sprint investment.

The this thinking implication of this addition is worth naming. EOS
gives leadership teams a language for operational clarity. Signal
extends that language into the AI investment conversation. The company
that can say \'our Q3 Sprint is targeted at a \$558K constraint and our
Deliver measurement will confirm whether we recovered it\' is having a
different quality of AI conversation than the company still discussing
which tools to buy. The EOS discipline is the foundation. Signal is the
extension that makes it work in the AI context.

One practical guidance for EOS companies: bring the Signal process into
the quarterly offsite as a dedicated session rather than running it as
an add-on to an existing meeting. The constraint identification work is
deep enough and political enough that it deserves its own uninterrupted
time. Two hours of proper Signal work at the start of a quarterly
offsite produces a sprint commitment that the entire leadership team
feels ownership of. That ownership is the this methodology condition
that makes the sprint succeed.

**4.7 The Failure Mode That Looks Like Work**

The Signal failure mode that is hardest to catch in real time is the
team that works hard, produces a lot of output, and still does not
complete Signal. It looks like progress because the activity level is
high. But the output is not the Signal deliverable: it is a collection
of related work that feels like Signal but has not produced a
one-sentence constraint statement with a validated cost and a leadership
team commitment.

This failure mode has a specific organizational dynamic behind it.
Leadership teams that are uncomfortable with the political act of
picking one constraint often manage that discomfort by expanding the
scope of the diagnostic work. The more they analyze, the longer they can
defer the commitment. The analysis provides cover for the avoidance. The
facilitator\'s job is to name that pattern when it appears and return
the team to the question: what is the one constraint, and what does it
cost?

From an organizational design perspective, this failure mode is a form
of structural avoidance: using procedural activity to delay a structural
decision. Every organization has developed its own version of this
pattern over time, and Signal will encounter it. Knowing that it is
coming, recognizing it when it appears, and holding the process to the
deliverable rather than the activity is the difference between a Signal
session that produces a committed constraint and one that produces a
comprehensive issues analysis that everyone agrees is insightful and
nobody commits to acting on.

**4.8 With the Constraint Locked, You Can Map What Surrounds It**

Signal produces one sentence and a validated number. Source turns that
sentence into a map of everything the organization knows about the
problem that sentence names. The relationship between the two is
sequential and structural: you cannot map what you have not diagnosed,
and you cannot design a solution to a map you have not drawn.

When Signal hands off to Source, it hands off three things. The
constraint statement itself: the one sentence naming the structural
cause, its location in the organization, its duration, and its cost. The
Is/Is Not pattern from the constraint identification process: the
boundary conditions that tell Source where to look and where not to
look. And the leadership team\'s commitment: the agreement that this is
the constraint worth a quarter of focused effort, which creates the
organizational permission Source needs to access the people and systems
that hold the relevant knowledge.

That permission is not a formality. In every the work design engagement,
access to the people who hold institutional knowledge is contingent on
visible leadership support. If the team doing Source arrives in a
conversation with a key knowledge holder without having clearly
communicated that this is a leadership-sponsored initiative, the
knowledge holder responds with polite, partial answers. The full
picture, the one that reveals the real constraint and the real knowledge
gaps, only emerges when the knowledge holder believes the information
they share will be used to build something real, not to produce a report
that gets filed and forgotten.

**5. Source**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Source is the stage that separates organizations that have done Signal
from organizations that have done Signal well enough to design
something. The constraint is named. The cost is validated. The Human
Orchestrator is confirmed. What Source produces is the map of everything
the organization actually knows about the structural problem that
constraint statement describes. Not what the organization thinks it
knows. Not what it has documented. What it actually knows, where that
knowledge lives, who holds it, in what form it is accessible, and what
is missing.

The the structural framework framing for Source is this: every AI sprint
is attempting to build a system that can reliably do work the
organization currently does through human judgment, human memory, and
human relationships. Before any of that work can be transferred to an AI
system, it has to be visible. Source makes the invisible visible. The
knowledge that exists in tribal form, in individual heads, in
undocumented practices, in informal relationships, in the institutional
memory of people who have been in the organization for ten years, all of
that is organizational capital that the sprint needs and that no agent
can access until it has been surfaced and structured.

The this practice consequence of skipping Source is not technical
failure in Build. It is a working system that does the visible part of
the work and fails on the invisible part. The agent handles the standard
cases correctly and mishandles every exception, every edge case, every
situation that required the knowledge that Source would have captured
but did not. Those failures look like AI failures. They are
organizational design failures: the work was not fully mapped before the
system was built.

**5.1 Diagnosis Is Not Done Until You Know What You Have**

Signal tells you what the constraint is. Source tells you what the
organization has to work with in addressing it. The diagnostic is
incomplete without both. Most sprint failures that trace back to Source
are failures of completeness, not failures of effort. The team ran
Source. They captured the obvious knowledge. They built against what
they found. And then the system hit an edge case that nobody had
anticipated because nobody had thought to ask about it.

The TML framework gives Source its categorization logic and its
completeness standard. Task knowledge is the level at which most Source
work gets done: the documented steps, the SOPs, the process maps, the
things that are already written down. Management knowledge is the level
at which Source work gets harder: the decision rights, the escalation
paths, the governance rules, the things that are partially documented
but followed inconsistently. Leadership knowledge is the level at which
Source work gets hardest: the judgment, the relationships, the context,
the pattern recognition that only comes from years inside the business.
Most Source processes stop at the Task level. Complete Source reaches
the Management and Leadership levels as well.

The the accountability structure implication is direct: the agent team
that is only trained on Task knowledge will perform correctly in the
standard cases and incorrectly in the Management and Leadership cases.
If the standard cases represent eighty percent of the workflow volume
and the Management and Leadership cases represent twenty percent, the
agent handles eighty percent correctly. But that twenty percent is
almost always the most consequential work in the workflow: the
exceptions, the high-value accounts, the situations where getting it
wrong costs the most. Source exists to make that twenty percent visible
before Build begins.

**5.2 Create a Knowledge Map**

The Knowledge Map is the one-page Source deliverable. It shows
everything the organization knows about the validated constraint across
three sections: Digital sources (data, systems, documents), Organic
sources (people and their knowledge), and Missing sources (knowledge
that the sprint needs but does not currently exist in any accessible
form).

The discipline of completing all three sections is the the design work
discipline of Source. Most teams naturally invest time in the Digital
section and the Organic section. They document what exists. The Missing
sources section is where the design work that saves the sprint happens.
Every missing source is a decision the sprint team has to make: is this
knowledge recoverable (can we capture it before Build begins?),
manageable (can we design the system to flag situations where the
missing knowledge would be needed and escalate to a human?), or
constraining (is the absence of this knowledge a reason to reconsider
the sprint design or the sprint constraint itself?).

An organizational design principle applies here directly: you cannot
build a system for work you do not understand. The Missing sources
section is the honest acknowledgment of what you do not yet understand.
Teams that skip it or leave it blank are not doing better Source work.
They are doing less honest Source work. The missing knowledge will
surface in production, where the cost of discovering it is significantly
higher than the cost of naming it during Source.

**5.3 Classify What You Find**

Once the Knowledge Map is populated, every source is classified against
the three AI tiers introduced in Chapter 2. This classification drives
the Design decisions about which connection pattern each agent team will
require and what the Build will need to construct.

The this discipline frame for this classification is not technical. It
is an accountability question: who is responsible for ensuring that each
tier of knowledge is connected to the agent team before Build begins?
Skills-on-SOPs requires someone to own the documentation of the tribal
knowledge that will become the agent\'s context. RAG requires someone to
own the curation of the document corpus and the decisions about what is
included, excluded, and how frequently it is updated. Data pipelines
require someone to own the technical connections between systems and to
govern what the agent can and cannot access.

In most organizations, nobody currently owns any of these three things
as a named accountability. They are emergent, not designed. Source is
the stage where those accountabilities are named for the first time, and
where the Human Orchestrator takes on the responsibility for ensuring
that the knowledge layer the sprint requires is built before Build
begins. The agent is only as good as the knowledge it can access. The
knowledge it can access is only as good as the organizational
accountability for keeping it current. Source is where that
accountability is first assigned.

**5.4 How Systems Talk to Each Other**

The technical architecture of how systems connect to each other is
covered in detail in Chapter 6. What Source establishes is the map of
which systems hold the relevant knowledge and which connections
currently exist or need to be built. This is not a technical exercise.
It is an organizational exercise: the Human Orchestrator, who is not
required to be a technical person, needs to understand which of their
organization\'s systems hold pieces of the constraint workflow\'s data,
whether those systems currently communicate with each other, and what
the business consequences are of any gaps in that communication.

The the design lens framing for this exercise is the information flow
question: how does the relevant information currently move through the
organization, and where does it get stuck? Every place it gets stuck is
a potential target for the agent workflow. Every connection that
currently requires a human to manually transfer information from one
system to another is a workflow automation opportunity. The Source
mapping of system connectivity is not about infrastructure. It is about
identifying where the organization is currently paying human judgment to
do information routing, when information routing is exactly the kind of
work agents can do reliably and at high volume.

**5.5 People Do Not Have APIs**

The organizational design insight behind this section heading is worth
unpacking before the practical guidance. An API is a defined interface:
a structured way to query a system and receive a structured response.
Systems with APIs can be connected to other systems programmatically,
without human intervention at each query. People do not have that
interface. To access what a person knows, you have to create the
connection manually: interview them, observe them, ask them to walk you
through their process, record their decisions and their rationale. That
process is slow, expensive, and imperfect. It is also irreplaceable.

The organizational consequence of the absence of an API is that every
piece of knowledge that lives only in a person\'s head is a single point
of failure in the organizational system. When that person is present,
the system works. When they are absent, on vacation, ill, in a meeting,
or departed, the knowledge is inaccessible. Source addresses this by
creating a structured substitute for the API: interviews, transcripts,
documented decision trees, and captured exception protocols that can be
stored in an agent\'s knowledge layer and accessed at runtime instead of
requiring the person to be present.

The people leader\'s role in this process is to create the
organizational conditions under which that knowledge capture can happen.
That means communicating to knowledge holders why their knowledge
matters and why the organization is investing in capturing it. It means
protecting the time required for Source interviews in a calendar
environment where every hour is already committed to operations. And it
means managing the human dimension of the knowledge capture
conversation: some people experience the documentation of their
institutional knowledge as a threat to their organizational value, not
as a contribution to organizational resilience. That perception has to
be actively addressed, not assumed away.

**5.6 Transcripts: The Closest Thing a Person Has to an API**

The structured interview and its transcript is the practical mechanism
for creating a person\'s closest approximation to an API. The goal of
the Source interview is not to create a procedure manual. It is to
surface the decision logic: the rules the person follows, the exceptions
they navigate, the edge cases they handle, and the judgment calls they
make that are not written down anywhere because they have never needed
to be. That is exactly the knowledge an agent needs to perform reliably
in the situations where the standard rules do not apply.

Interview design matters for the quality of what Source produces. The
most common Source interview failure is a conversation that stays at the
Task level: walk me through your process. What do you do first, then
second, then third? That conversation produces a procedure map. It does
not produce the Management-level decision logic that determines which
path to take when the procedure encounters an exception. Reaching the
Management level requires a different question type: what would cause
you to do something different at that step? What does the situation look
like when you have to escalate? What information would change your
decision? Those questions surface the decision logic that the agent will
need to handle the twenty percent of situations that the procedure map
does not cover.

**5.6.1 Sprint: Source at Meridian**

At Meridian, the Source exercise uncovered three levels of knowledge. At
the Task level: the standard quoting procedure was documented in enough
detail to train an agent on the mechanics. At the Management level:
Elena\'s decision logic for pricing exceptions, lead-time adjustments
for certain material categories, and the treatment of RFQs from
customers with custom specifications required three additional interview
sessions to surface fully. At the Leadership level: Elena\'s knowledge
of which customers were price-sensitive versus schedule-sensitive, her
sense of which RFQs represented relationship investments versus pure
commercial transactions, and her judgment about when to negotiate rather
than quote all represented knowledge that could not be systematized
directly but could be referenced in the agent\'s escalation logic.

The Missing sources section identified two critical gaps. There was no
formal lead-time database. Elena was pulling lead-time estimates from
memory and from periodic conversations with Dave. The sprint needed that
knowledge in a queryable system before Build could produce a reliable
quoting agent. And there was no documented exception-handling protocol.
Elena made exception decisions consistently based on her experience, but
those decisions were not written down anywhere. The sprint could not
produce a fully functional quoting agent until at least the most common
exception categories were documented and converted into agent decision
rules.

Both gaps were addressed before Design began. The lead-time data was
captured in a structured spreadsheet and connected to the agent\'s
knowledge layer. The exception protocol was developed in two working
sessions with Elena, producing twenty-three documented exception
categories covering ninety-one percent of the exceptions she handled in
the prior two quarters. The sprint did not wait for complete data. It
proceeded with enough data to build against, with the missing data
flagged in the HAC governance framework as categories requiring Elena\'s
personal review rather than agent handling.

**5.7 Knowledge Management in an Agentic World**

Source forces a conversation that most organizations have been
deferring: what happens to our organizational knowledge when someone
leaves? In a world where all workers are human, the departure of a
high-knowledge person is a painful but manageable event. The
organization rebuilds the knowledge through institutional memory,
informal knowledge transfer, and the slow accumulation of experience by
the person\'s replacement. That process takes months to years and
produces some knowledge loss, but it is generally survivable.

In an agentic world, the consequences of unmanaged knowledge are more
severe. An agent team that is built against tribal knowledge is an agent
team that will degrade when the person who held that tribal knowledge is
no longer available to correct it. If the knowledge layer is not
systematically maintained as the organization evolves, the agent team\'s
outputs will drift from accurate to plausible-but-wrong in ways that are
difficult to detect until the damage is done. The agent will still
produce outputs. They will just be increasingly wrong in ways that look
right.

This is the this field argument for treating Source as a standing
discipline rather than a one-time sprint artifact. The Knowledge Map
that Source produces is a living document. It should be updated every
time a high-knowledge person transitions, every time a system changes,
every time a process is redesigned. The Human Orchestrator owns that
update responsibility. The quarterly operating session includes a
standing Knowledge Map review as part of the Compound stage\'s living
documents update. Organizations that treat Source as a one-time artifact
and organizations that treat it as a standing discipline will diverge in
their sprint outcomes over time. The difference shows up by sprint four
or five: the organization with a maintained Knowledge Map runs faster
Source sessions because most of the map already exists. The organization
with a stale or missing map runs Source from scratch every quarter.

**5.8 The Completeness Test**

Before calling Source done, run six checks. Every digital source in the
Knowledge Map has a noted status: Clean (the data is current,
accessible, and structured), Needs Work (the data exists but requires
curation, cleaning, or restructuring before it can serve as an agent
knowledge layer), or Missing (the data does not exist in any accessible
form and needs to be created or captured).

Every organic source has been interviewed. Not emailed a survey.
Interviewed. The difference matters. Surveys produce the knowledge that
people know they have and know they have permission to share. Interviews
surface the knowledge that people do not know they have and would not
think to document: the habitual decisions, the informal rules, the
workarounds that have become standard practice without anyone having
deliberately decided to standardize them.

Every missing source has a named owner and a specific plan: either a
commitment to fill the gap before Design begins, or a decision to
proceed with the gap acknowledged and noted in the HAC governance
framework as an escalation trigger. Every at-risk source has been
flagged and a capture plan is in progress. The Knowledge Map fits on one
page: if it does not fit on one page, it has not been synthesized to the
constraint level and more curation is needed. The Human Orchestrator can
describe what the map shows to someone who was not in the Source
sessions: if they cannot, the map is not yet operational knowledge.

**5.9 Hand Off to Design**

Source hands three things to Design. The completed Knowledge Map: the
organizational knowledge inventory the sprint will build against. The
connection pattern for each agent team: Skills-on-SOPs, RAG, Data
Pipeline, or Agentic with RAG. And the gap register: the missing sources
that will either be filled before Design is complete or will be
designated as human-judgment-required in the Work Deconstruction.

Design cannot make accurate work allocation decisions without all three.
A Work Deconstruction that allocates tasks to an agent without knowing
what knowledge that agent will have access to is a design that will fail
when Build deploys it against reality. The Design Gate, the checklist
that must be passed before Build begins, includes a check for Source
completeness: if Source is not complete, Design is not complete, and
Build cannot begin. The gate is real.

**6. Designing the System**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Design is where the operating discipline becomes explicit. Everything
Signal and Source produced is input. The output of Design is a
structure: who owns what, what the agent does, what the human does, how
those two things connect, and what happens when the boundary between
them is crossed incorrectly. That structure is the organizational design
artifact that makes the sprint result permanent.

Every AI implementation that has ever failed has failed for one of two
reasons. It was built before the problem was diagnosed (the Signal and
Source failure), or it was built without an explicit ownership structure
that held through deployment (the Design failure). The second failure
mode is less visible than the first because the system technically works
at launch. The failure happens gradually, as the accountability gaps
that Design left unfilled begin to accumulate: the agent team that
nobody is reviewing regularly, the escalation protocol that nobody
follows because it was never socialized, the edge cases that nobody is
catching because the governance framework was too vague to create the
habit of catching them.

Design prevents that failure mode by forcing the organization to make
every accountability decision explicitly before a line of code is
written. Not approximately. Not implied. Explicitly: this function is
owned by this named human, this agent team operates at this level of
autonomy, this situation triggers this escalation, this condition
triggers this kill switch. The the systematic approach discipline behind
Design is the same discipline behind any accountability structure:
unowned work fails, ambiguous accountability produces inconsistent
execution, and the structures that get documented and owned are the
structures that hold.

**6.1 What Design Produces**

Design produces two deliverables that must ship together. Shipping one
without the other is not partial completion. It is a design that will
fail at a predictable point.

The Hybrid Accountability Chart (HAC) is the ownership document. It
names every function in the constraint workflow, the agent team or human
who executes it, and the human supervisor who owns the outcome. Every
row is complete: no unnamed supervisors, no agent teams without a human
responsible for their output, no functions that exist in the HAC without
a clear accountability assignment. The HAC is not a technical document.
It is an organizational document that happens to include AI agents
alongside humans in the same accountability structure the organization
uses to govern all its work.

The Design Brief is the implementation document. It captures every
design decision in writing: workflow summary, stakeholders, systems
involved, data requirements, success criteria, constraints and
guardrails, and the V1 artifact description. The Design Brief is the
contract between the Design stage and the Build stage. A Build that
proceeds without a completed Design Brief is a Build that has been given
permission to make undisclosed design decisions. Those decisions will
surface in production, where they are expensive to correct.

**6.2 The Hybrid Accountability Chart**

The HAC extends a concept that every leadership team in an EOS or
Scaling Up company already uses: the accountability chart. The
traditional accountability chart names functions and assigns humans to
them. The HAC does the same thing, with one extension: agent teams are
included as named entities in the chart, with the same governance
expectations that apply to any other function.

The this kind of work argument for this extension is not about AI
governance in the abstract. It is about operational accountability in
the specific. When an agent team produces an output that causes a
problem, whether that is a quote with an incorrect material estimate, a
customer communication that misrepresents a delivery commitment, or a
report that surfaces misleading data, the question that the HAC must be
able to answer is: who is responsible? The answer cannot be \'the AI.\'
The AI is not an organizational actor that can be held responsible. The
human supervisor named in the HAC entry for that agent team is the
person responsible. The HAC makes that accountability visible before the
problem occurs, not after.

The HAC builds on the Right Seat Evaluation framework. Every supervisor
named in the HAC must See It: they must have enough domain knowledge to
evaluate whether the agent\'s output is correct. They must Want It: they
must be genuinely accountable for the outcome, not just named for
organizational optics. And they must be Suited for It: they must have
the judgment, authority, and operational bandwidth to make the calls the
agent cannot make and to catch the errors the agent will make. A
supervisor who does not pass these three tests is not a real supervisor
in the HAC. They are a placeholder, and the work that placeholder covers
is unowned work that will fail the same way unowned work always fails.

**6.3 The Information Flow Question**

Before the HAC can be fully populated, the Design team needs to map how
information moves through the constraint workflow. This is not a
technical exercise, though it has technical implications. It is an
organizational design exercise: the question is not how the systems are
architected but how the work is done.

The information flow question produces four answers. What data feeds the
work, and which systems or people hold it? This answer tells Design
where the agent team\'s knowledge layer needs to connect and who owns
the access decisions for each connection. What transformations happen to
that data as it moves through the workflow? This answer tells Design
which steps in the workflow are rule-based transformations
(agent-appropriate) and which require judgment about how to transform
the data (human-required or agent-assisted). What decisions get made
along the way, and whether each is rule-based or judgment-based? This
answer is the most critical input to Work Deconstruction in Chapter 7:
the classification of every decision in the workflow against the TML
framework determines what the agent can own and what the human must own.
What is the output, and where does it go next? This answer tells Design
what the done condition is for each agent team and who receives and
reviews the output.

The the structural work principle that makes this mapping exercise
valuable is the principle of explicit handoffs. Every transition between
actors in the workflow is a potential failure point: the place where
work can fall into the gap between who was supposed to hand it off and
who was supposed to receive it. Design\'s job is to eliminate that
ambiguity for every handoff in the constraint workflow before Build
constructs the system that will be responsible for making those handoffs
reliably.

**6.4 How AI Connects to Information**

The Source classification (Skills-on-SOPs, RAG, Data Pipelines, Service
Layers) drives a Design decision that has organizational accountability
implications: who is responsible for each connection? Skills-on-SOPs
requires someone to own the documentation that becomes the agent\'s
context. That ownership means ensuring the documentation is current,
accurate, and complete enough to produce reliable agent outputs. RAG
requires someone to own the knowledge base: decisions about what is
included, how frequently it is updated, and how the retrieval quality is
monitored over time. Data pipelines require someone to own the
integrations: ensuring the connections are live, the data flowing
through them is clean, and the access governance is maintained.

In most organizations at the start of a sprint, nobody currently owns
any of these things as a named accountability. They are about to become
named accountabilities in the HAC. The Design stage is where those
ownership decisions are made for the first time, and where the Human
Orchestrator takes on responsibility for the knowledge layer as a
component of the agent team\'s performance. The this thinking
implication: the Human Orchestrator\'s job is not just to review the
agent\'s outputs. It is to maintain the knowledge layer that enables the
agent to produce correct outputs in the first place. That maintenance
responsibility should be visible in the Human Orchestrator\'s role
definition before the sprint launches.

**6.5 AI-Assisted or Automated**

The AI-Assisted versus Automated decision is one of the most
consequential accountability decisions in the Design stage, and it is
frequently made too quickly or with insufficient organizational context.

AI-Assisted means the agent produces an output and a human reviews it
before it has any organizational consequence. The human is in the loop
at every execution, not just at the exception level. The agent\'s output
triggers a human review step. The human makes the go/no-go decision.
This is appropriate for workflows where the cost of an error is high,
where the output is externally visible, or where the human reviewer can
add judgment value that the agent cannot supply, such as relationship
context, strategic alignment, or reputational awareness.

Automated means the agent\'s output executes without human review, with
a human monitoring by exception. The agent runs. The Human Orchestrator
reviews aggregated performance data, exception flags, and output quality
metrics on a defined cadence, rather than reviewing individual outputs.
This is appropriate for high-volume workflows where the cost of
individual errors is low and recoverable, where the output is internal,
and where full human review of every output is not operationally
feasible.

The organizational design error that most commonly occurs in this
decision is Automated by default: the team chooses Automated because it
feels more ambitious, or because the Human Orchestrator does not want to
commit to a high-volume daily review, without adequately analyzing what
the cost of errors in that workflow actually is. The Right Seat
Evaluation applies here directly: does the Human Orchestrator truly See
the work well enough to monitor by exception? A Human Orchestrator who
does not deeply understand the workflow\'s exception patterns cannot
monitor by exception effectively. They are not monitoring. They are
hoping.

The practical guidance from this methodology practice: start at
AI-Assisted for all workflows. Move to Automated only after the Human
Orchestrator has reviewed enough individual outputs to develop a
reliable exception pattern and to confirm that the agent\'s error rate
in the current operational environment is low enough to justify reducing
the review frequency. This is not conservative for its own sake. It is
the the work design equivalent of the probationary period: a new
process, like a new employee, earns increased autonomy through
demonstrated performance, not through the assumption of performance
before it has been established.

**6.6 Governance and Guardrails**

Guardrails are not technical restrictions added to a system after the
design is complete. They are organizational decisions made before the
design is finalized about what the system will and will not do. The
distinction matters because guardrails that are added after the fact
tend to be reactive: they are triggered by a problem that has already
occurred. Guardrails that are designed in advance are proactive: they
define the conditions under which the system should stop and return to a
human before those conditions are encountered in production.

The organizational design frame for guardrails is risk management
applied to workflow design. Every workflow has a set of scenarios where
the stakes are high enough, or the situation is unusual enough, that the
organization should not want an agent operating without human oversight.
Guardrails name those scenarios explicitly and define what the agent
does when it encounters them. The agent does not have to be intelligent
enough to recognize that it is in a high-stakes situation. The
guardrails define the conditions in advance, and the agent checks
against those conditions at runtime.

The five guardrail questions that every sprint Design must answer are
not a bureaucratic checklist. Each one maps to a specific organizational
accountability. What data can agents access, and what is blocked at the
source? This is a data governance decision that should involve the
organization\'s information security perspective, not just the sprint
team\'s operational needs. What actions can agents take without
approval, and which require human sign-off? This is a delegation
decision: at what point does the organizational authority to act require
a human decision-maker rather than an automated system? What happens
when an agent encounters something outside its design? This is a failure
management decision: how does the organization want to handle the
situations its agent was not built for? How will output quality be
monitored? This is a quality management decision: what constitutes
acceptable performance, and how will the organization detect when
performance falls below that level? What would cause the team to shut
down the workflow immediately? This is a risk management decision: what
are the conditions under which the organization prefers operational
disruption to continued automated operation?

**6.7 The Human Orchestrator**

The Human Orchestrator is the most important this approach decision in
the sprint. The HAC can have perfect accountability assignments, the
Design Brief can be comprehensive, and the guardrails can be
well-designed. If the Human Orchestrator is not the right person, none
of that holds in production.

The the structural framework argument for why this role is so
consequential is rooted in the nature of AI systems in operational
environments. Agent teams do not improve or degrade themselves. They
perform against their design. When the real-world environment diverges
from the designed environment (and it always does, eventually), the
agent does not adapt. It continues performing against the original
design. The Human Orchestrator is the person responsible for detecting
that divergence and deciding whether the design needs to be updated.

That role requires three capabilities that are organizational, not
technical. Domain knowledge: the ability to evaluate the agent\'s
outputs against the standard of what good looks like for that function.
Operational authority: the organizational standing to make decisions
about when the agent should escalate, when the design should change, and
when the workflow should stop. And sustained attention: the
organizational bandwidth to maintain active oversight of the agent team
on a regular cadence, not just when something goes visibly wrong.

The Right Seat Evaluation (Sees It, Wants It, Suited for It) is the
organizational design lens for this selection. Sees It means the person
has enough domain knowledge to recognize errors and exceptions without
needing to consult others. Wants It means the person experiences the
Human Orchestrator role as their own accountability, not as a borrowed
responsibility. Suited for It means the person has both the cognitive
capability to perform the oversight function and the organizational
positioning to act on what they observe. A Human Orchestrator who Sees
It and Wants It but is not Suited for It will be excellent at
identifying problems and powerless to resolve them. That is the most
demoralizing failure mode in the role.

**6.7.1 Finding the Right Person**

The Human Orchestrator is almost never the most senior person available.
The most senior person is typically the most removed from the work, the
least likely to See It in the domain-knowledge sense, and the most
likely to experience the oversight function as beneath their
organizational role. The Human Orchestrator is the person who is closest
to the work, who has the most direct knowledge of what good looks like,
and who has the organizational positioning to make the day-to-day
operational decisions the role requires.

At Meridian, Elena Ruiz was the right Human Orchestrator for the quoting
sprint not because she was the VP of Operations but because she was the
person who had been doing the quoting work. She knew every edge case.
She had made every exception decision. She could evaluate every agent
output against the standard of what a correct quote looks like. Her
title was relevant because it gave her the authority to change the
workflow when the design needed updating. But her domain knowledge was
the real qualification.

**6.8 The Design Team**

The Design Team is bounded for organizational reasons, not just
practical ones. Large Design teams produce designs by consensus, which
is a different thing from designs by clarity. A Design decision that has
been debated and compromised through a large group carries the political
compromises of that process into the Build stage, where they surface as
ambiguous requirements. A Design decision made by a small, focused team
with clear accountability is a decision that Build can act on.

The Design Team is the Human Orchestrator, the one or two people closest
to the constraint workflow from the operational side, and a technical
resource if the design calls for system integrations that require
engineering judgment. Three to five people total. The leadership sponsor
is present for the Design Gate decision but is not a member of the
day-to-day Design Team. Their presence at the Design Gate is an
accountability check: does the leadership sponsor agree that the design
produced by this team is the one they would choose if they had been in
every session?

Meridian\'s Design stage ran across three working sessions over two

entries. Session two: AI-Assisted versus Automated decisions and the
guardrails framework. Session three: Design Brief completion and Design
Gate review. Elena was present for all three. The technical resource
joined for sessions two and three.

**6.9.1 Hybrid Accountability Chart: Meridian Quoting Sprint**

**6.9.2 Human Roles After Design**

Elena\'s role changed in a specific way. The task allocation shifted:
quoting went from fifteen hours per week of direct execution to four
hours per week of review and exception handling. The eleven hours freed
by the design went into strategic operations work: capacity planning,
vendor relationship development, and the process improvement backlog
that had accumulated over two years while quoting consumed her time. Her
title did not change. Her organizational contribution did.

The Design stage documented that change explicitly, because the this
practice implication of recovering eleven hours of a VP\'s time is not
just operational efficiency. It is a statement about how the
organization is choosing to use its most valuable human capacity. The
sprint\'s value is not only the \$412K cost reduction in quoting. It is
also the strategic operations work that Elena can now do that was
previously inaccessible because the quoting function occupied the time
that work required.

**6.9.3 Guardrails**

Five guardrails were defined before Build began. No quote reaches a
customer without Elena\'s review: the agent produces drafts only. The
agent cannot access customer payment information or credit terms. Any
RFQ with specifications flagged as outside the standard parameter set
routes to Elena within the same business day. Elena reviews the agent\'s
output log every Monday morning. The kill switch: if the agent\'s
material estimate error rate exceeds five percent in any given week, the
workflow pauses and Elena handles quoting manually until the design is
corrected and retested.

**7. Designing the Work**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Designing the System (Chapter 6) produced the governance framework: who
owns what function, at what level of autonomy, with what guardrails.
Designing the Work goes deeper: it produces the task-level allocation of
every step in the constraint workflow between humans and agents, and it
produces the visual map that makes that allocation legible to everyone
who will be affected by the change.

The the accountability structure framing for this chapter is work
design, a discipline that predates AI by decades. Work design is the
explicit process of deciding how work should be organized, allocated,
and governed inside a specific operational context. It is distinct from
job design (defining what a role is responsible for) and from process
design (defining how a workflow is sequenced). Work design goes to the
level of individual tasks: what is each task, what type of intelligence
does it require, who or what is best suited to perform it, and how does
it connect to the tasks that precede and follow it?

Most organizations have never done explicit work design. Work is
organized by accretion: tasks are added to roles as the work evolves,
workflows are built around the people available to perform them, and the
accumulated result is a set of role definitions that reflect
organizational history more than organizational design. AI does not
change this pattern. It intensifies it. Organizations that add agent
capabilities to roles that were never designed in the first place add
more complexity to an already complex structure. Organizations that
redesign the work before adding agent capabilities produce a structure
that compounds because the design is deliberate.

**7.1 Work Deconstruction**

Work Deconstruction is the task-level equivalent of the the design work
process that produces an accountability chart. Where the accountability
chart assigns functions to organizational roles, Work Deconstruction
assigns tasks to types of intelligence. Every task in the constraint
workflow is examined against one question: what type of intelligence
does this task require to be performed reliably at the quality standard
this organization needs?

The TML framework provides the answer to that question in three
categories. Task work follows defined rules, operates on structured
data, and produces predictable outputs. The intelligence required is
pattern recognition and rule application: exactly what agents do well
and what they can do reliably at high volume without fatigue,
inconsistency, or quality degradation over time. Task work is the
primary target for full automation in a sprint design.

Management work requires judgment about process: deciding which rule
applies in a given situation, evaluating whether an output meets a
standard, determining when an exception warrants escalation, and
maintaining the quality standard over time. The intelligence required is
contextual judgment applied to a defined domain. This is the AI-Assisted
zone: the agent handles the execution, the human applies the judgment to
the output. Management work is the primary target for AI-Assisted
design.

Leadership work requires judgment about people, strategy, relationships,
ethics, and context that is irreducibly human. Not because agents lack
the capability to produce outputs in these domains, but because the
organizational accountability for those decisions cannot be transferred
to an agent. When a decision affects a relationship, a strategy, or the
organization\'s values, a human must own it. Not because agents cannot
reason about those domains, but because the organization cannot hold an
agent accountable for those decisions in any meaningful sense.
Leadership work stays with humans permanently.

**7.2 The Role, Deconstructed**

Work Deconstruction at the role level reveals something that most job
descriptions obscure: most roles contain work from all three TML
categories, often mixed in ways that were never deliberately designed. A
project coordinator who spends forty percent of their week on task-level
data entry and thirty percent on management-level exception handling and
thirty percent on leadership-level client relationship maintenance is
doing three different types of work that require three different types
of intelligence. The agent team that can handle the first forty percent
frees the human to move more deeply into the second and third
categories.

The this discipline implication of Work Deconstruction at the role level
is not simply efficiency improvement. It is role elevation: the designed
transfer of task-level work from humans to agents creates space for
humans to move up the TML stack into the work that requires more of what
makes them distinctively valuable. This is the Pattern Lift that the
Compound Sequence is designed to produce. Not just that a workflow runs
faster. That the humans in that workflow are doing more interesting,
more strategic, and more impactful work because the routine work that
was consuming their capacity has been transferred to systems designed to
handle it.

We ran Work Deconstruction on the project coordination role at Jesse\'s
company. Eighteen distinct tasks were identified. Three required human
judgment and were kept entirely with the Human Orchestrator. Four were
management-level and became agent-assisted: the agent produces the
output, the Human Orchestrator reviews it. Nine were task-level and
became fully automated: the agent runs without review, the Human
Orchestrator monitors by exception. Two were pure data routing and
required no intelligence at all, handled by workflow automation tools
without any language model involved.

The junior coordinator listing was never posted. One row in the Work
Deconstruction chart replaced a \$44,000 annual hire. Not because the
agent is cheaper than a person. Because the role that the organization
was about to hire for was a role designed around work that, properly
analyzed, did not require a human.

**7.2.1 Populated Example: Meridian Manufacturing Quoting**

Elena\'s quoting workflow contained twenty-two distinct tasks. The Work
Deconstruction classified them as follows. Two tasks required human
judgment: reviewing quotes for specifications that fell outside the
agent\'s decision parameters, and making pricing decisions for accounts
where the relationship context affected the standard rate. These stayed
entirely with Elena.

Four tasks were management-level and became agent-assisted: generating
the initial draft quote from RFQ inputs (agent drafts, Elena reviews),
flagging RFQs that fall outside standard parameters (agent flags, Elena
decides on handling), summarizing job history for repeat customers to
provide context for the quote (agent summarizes, Elena validates before
using), and communicating quote status updates to Ty in sales (agent
generates, Elena approves before sending).

Fourteen tasks were task-level and became fully automated: parsing RFQ
document format, looking up material pricing from the pricing database,
calculating standard labor hours from Dave\'s estimation tables,
assembling the draft quote template, cross-referencing the RFQ against
prior similar jobs for consistency, generating the quote reference
number, logging the quote in the CRM, updating quote status at each
stage of Elena\'s review, routing completed quotes to Ty for customer
delivery, generating weekly quote pipeline reports for Mark, archiving
completed and declined quotes, and three additional data validation and
formatting tasks.

Two tasks were workflow automation only: routing incoming RFQs from
email to the agent queue, and triggering follow-up reminders at defined
intervals after quote delivery. No language model required. Pure data
routing.

The result: Elena\'s direct quoting time dropped from fifteen hours per
week to four hours per week of review and exception handling. Dave\'s
time contribution to quoting dropped from three to four hours per week
of informal consultation to less than thirty minutes per week of
exception review. Ty\'s time on quote-related coordination dropped from
two hours per week to approximately fifteen minutes per week of
exception escalation.

**7.3 Seeing the Design**

The swim lane diagram makes the Work Deconstruction visible as an
organizational picture. One horizontal lane per actor: one lane for
Elena, one lane for the Quoting Agent team, one lane for the Material
Estimator agent, one lane for the workflow automation layer. Every task
in the constraint workflow appears in the diagram in the lane of the
actor responsible for it. Every crossing between lanes is a handoff: the
moment at which one actor\'s work ends and another\'s begins.

The organizational design value of the swim lane diagram is that it
makes handoffs visible as a design object rather than as an operational
assumption. In most organizations, handoffs are informal: the previous
actor somehow signals to the next actor that their work is ready. That
signal is often implicit (an email arriving, a task status changing, a
Slack message sent), and the implicit signal is the source of most
handoff failures: the signal was sent, the receiving actor did not
notice, and the work stopped in the gap.

The swim lane diagram forces every handoff to be explicit: this step
produces this output, it moves to this actor via this signal, that actor
does this with it. Designing the handoffs before Build constructs the
system that executes them is the the design lens practice that prevents
the failure mode where the system technically executes the handoff but
the human on the receiving end does not know they have received
something or does not know what to do with it.

**7.4 The Design Brief**

The Design Brief is the written record of every design decision made in
Chapters 6 and 7. Its value is not primarily as documentation. Its value
is as a forcing function: writing the Design Brief requires the Design
Team to convert every design conversation into a decision. Conversations
produce shared understanding. Decisions produce accountable commitments.
The Design Brief is the artifact that converts the Design Team\'s shared
understanding into the commitments that Build will execute against.

The Design Brief contains eight sections. Workflow summary: what the
workflow does in one paragraph, written plainly enough that someone who
was not in any Design session can understand it. Stakeholders: every
role whose work changes as a result of the sprint, and how it changes.
Systems involved: every software system that the workflow touches, with
notes on the current state of connectivity and the access permissions
required. Data requirements: what specific data the agent teams need to
produce correct outputs, where that data currently lives, and what
condition it is in. Success criteria: the measurable outcome that
defines a successful sprint, drawn directly from Signal\'s cost
estimate. Constraints and guardrails: all five guardrail decisions from
Chapter 6, plus any operational constraints identified during Source
(such as the categories of work that will be flagged for human review
rather than automated). V1 artifact description: what specifically will
be built in Build, with enough specificity that a technical resource who
reads this section knows what they are building without asking the
Design Team for clarification. Autonomy level and progression: the
starting AI-Assisted or Automated designation for each agent team, plus
the milestones that would justify moving toward greater autonomy.

The Design Brief is the handoff document from Design to Build. Build can
read it and know exactly what to build, in what systems, with what
governance, against what success criteria, without convening additional
Design sessions. If the Design Brief cannot be written without more
design work, more design work needs to happen before Build begins.

**7.5 Prototype Before You Build**

The prototype is the most underutilized step in the Design stage, and it
is the step that catches the most expensive errors before they become
Build failures.

Prototyping an agent workflow in a conversational interface means
running the workflow manually, with a human simulating the agent\'s
role: pasting the relevant context into Claude or ChatGPT, running the
task, evaluating the output, identifying the gaps. It takes hours. What
it reveals is always more than the Design Team expected. The edge case
the team assumed was rare turns out to appear in the first ten test
cases. The knowledge the team assumed was sufficient to produce correct
outputs turns out to be ambiguous in ways that produce inconsistent
results. The output format the team designed is technically correct but
practically unusable because the downstream actor cannot easily apply
it.

The this field value of prototyping is that it converts assumptions into
observable data. Every Design session produces assumptions: we assume
the agent can do this, we assume this knowledge is sufficient, we assume
this output format will work for Elena. Prototyping tests those
assumptions before Build commits to them. The assumptions that fail in
the prototype are the Design gaps that would have become Build failures,
which would have become Deliver failures, which would have become sprint
failures. Prototyping in hours is always cheaper than debugging in
production.

**7.6 Design Is the Gate**

The Design Gate is the organizational accountability checkpoint that
ensures the sprint is ready to move from Design into Build. It is a
checklist, not a conversation. Every item either passes or fails.
Nothing advances until all items pass.

The five Design Gate items reflect the five organizational design
commitments that must be in place before Build can produce a reliable
system. Work Deconstruction is complete for every task in the constraint
workflow: every task has been classified, every classification has a
rationale, and every task in the Human Judgment Required category has a
named human responsible for it. Every accountability has a Hybrid
Accountability Chart entry with all four fields completed and a named
supervisor: no unnamed supervisors, no agent teams without human
accountability, no functions that exist in the Design without a
corresponding HAC entry. The Human Orchestrator is named, with
irreducible decisions and escalation triggers documented: not just a
name, but a clear description of what Elena (or whoever) is accountable
for reviewing, what conditions trigger an escalation, and what the
escalation path is. AI-Assisted versus Automated is decided for each
agent team, with a rationale written down: not a checkbox, a sentence
that explains why this workflow at this stage of the sprint justifies
this level of autonomy. Guardrails are defined for every agent team,
including the kill switch condition: five answers, all written, all
actionable.

The the operating discipline argument for the gate is the same argument
for every quality gate in an organizational process: the cost of
catching a problem increases with every stage that passes before it is
caught. A problem caught at the Design Gate costs a revised Design
Brief. The same problem caught in Build costs a rebuilt workflow. The
same problem caught in Deliver costs a failed sprint. The gate is not
bureaucratic caution. It is organizational efficiency: the most
cost-effective place to catch a design problem is before it is built
into a system.

**8. Build**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Build translates the locked Design into a working, deployed workflow.
The organizational design work is done. The accountability is assigned.
The governance is documented. Build\'s job is to construct the technical
system that executes the this kind of work the sprint team produced in
Chapters 6 and 7.

The most important thing to understand about Build from an
organizational perspective is what it is not. It is not the place where
design decisions are made. It is not the place where accountability is
assigned. It is not the place where governance is determined. Those
decisions were made in Design, and they are documented in the Design
Brief and the HAC. Build executes against those documents. When Build
encounters a situation that the Design Brief did not cover, Build does
not make a design decision. Build surfaces the gap to the Design Team
and waits for a documented decision before proceeding.

This discipline is harder to maintain than it sounds, because builders
are problem-solvers by nature. When a Build encounters a gap in the
Design Brief, the natural response is to fill it with the most
reasonable available assumption. That assumption becomes embedded in the
system. It is not documented in the Design Brief. It is not reflected in
the HAC. It is not reviewed in the Design Gate. And when it fails in
production, nobody knows it was a design assumption rather than a design
decision, and nobody knows where it came from or who made it.

The the structural work solution is cultural before it is procedural:
Build teams that understand why the Design Brief is authoritative, and
that experience being sent back to Design when they encounter a gap,
learn to surface gaps before they assume their way past them. That
learning happens through the first few Build sessions. Build teams that
have never experienced being sent back to Design make assumptions freely
and produce systems that fail at the points where those assumptions were
wrong.

**8.1 What Build Produces**

Build produces one thing: a working, deployed solution to the validated
constraint. Not a proof of concept. Not a demo. Not a workflow that
works in the sandbox with clean data. A workflow deployed in the systems
the team actually uses, validated against real operational data, with
the guardrails active, the Human Orchestrator able to run it
independently, and the operational log in place before the first live
input is processed.

The word \'deployed\' is load-bearing. A workflow that exists in a
development environment is not deployed. A workflow that the builder can
run is not deployed. Deployed means the workflow is live in the
production environment where it will run every day, accessible to the
Human Orchestrator without the builder\'s assistance, and producing
outputs that enter the organization\'s actual operational flow rather
than a test environment. Anything short of that is not Build complete.

**8.2 Build Is Scoped to the Designed Workflow**

The most common Build failure mode is scope expansion. The sprint was
designed to solve one constraint. Two weeks into Build, the team
identifies three adjacent problems the solution could also address. The
agent can also handle this, and this, and this. The scope expands. The
timeline extends. The original constraint, validated in Signal and
designed in Chapters 6 and 7, gets diluted by adjacent work that was not
validated and not designed.

The organizational design argument for holding the scope is not about
resource efficiency alone. It is about measurement integrity. A sprint
produces a measurable result against a named baseline. The Deliver
measurement compares the post-sprint operational state against Signal\'s
cost estimate. If the scope expanded during Build, the Deliver
measurement cannot cleanly attribute the operational changes to the
sprint design. The signal-to-noise ratio in the measurement decreases.
The leadership team cannot clearly answer whether the methodology worked
because the sprint did not cleanly test the methodology: it tested the
methodology plus two adjacent experiments that were added without Design
oversight.

Adjacent opportunities go on the Signal Backlog and become candidates
for the next sprint. They receive proper Signal treatment (constraint
validation, cost quantification) and proper Design treatment (work
deconstruction, HAC entry, Design Brief) in their own sprint. Not
because the adjacent opportunities are unimportant, but because the
organizational integrity of the sprint methodology requires each sprint
to test one hypothesis against one validated baseline.

**8.3 Build Lives Outside IT**

The most organizational significant characteristic of the Compound Build
stage is that most sprints do not require a software development team.
The tools available today, n8n, Make, Zapier, Claude Projects, Airtable,
Notion, and their equivalents, allow non-developers to build
sophisticated agent workflows without writing code.

This matters enormously for the organizational dynamics of the sprint.
In traditional IT project management, the technical build is in a
separate organizational silo from the business operations. The business
team designs what it wants. The IT team builds what it understood the
business to want. The translation between what was designed and what was
built introduces error at every handoff. The Deliver measurement reveals
those errors after the fact, when the cost of correction is highest.

When the Human Orchestrator can build the sprint alongside the technical
resource, or in some cases build it themselves, the translation errors
disappear. The person who knows the workflow best is present in the
Build sessions. When the technical resource makes an assumption about
how a guardrail should be implemented, the Human Orchestrator is in the
room to correct it. When the Build produces an output that technically
matches the Design Brief but practically does not match Elena\'s sense
of what a correct quote looks like, Elena is present to name that gap
and iterate on it before deployment.

**8.4 The Complexity Spectrum**

Sprint builds fall along a complexity spectrum that ranges from a few
hours of work to several weeks of engineering effort. The appropriate
point on the spectrum for a given sprint is determined by the Design
Brief, not by the available technical resources or the team\'s ambition
for what the sprint could produce.

At the low end: a structured Claude Project or a carefully designed
prompt architecture that the Human Orchestrator runs conversationally.
No integrations. No code. The agent exists in a chat interface. The
Human Orchestrator types inputs, receives outputs, reviews them, and
acts on them. This is a legitimate sprint result for workflows where the
constraint is the quality of the output rather than the volume or
automation of its production. It is also the right starting point for
first sprints with teams that have never built an agent workflow before.

At the middle: a workflow automation using Zapier, Make, or n8n that
connects two or three systems and incorporates a language model at one
or more steps to handle judgment-requiring tasks. Deployable in days to
a week. Appropriate for constraints where the volume is high enough that
the Human Orchestrator cannot review every output individually and the
AI-Assisted design needs to be supplemented by automated data routing.

At the high end: a multi-agent system with custom integrations,
requiring API build, engineered prompt architecture, and a technical
resource with development expertise. Deployable in two to four weeks
with proper scoping. Appropriate for constraints where the workflow is
complex, the volume is high, the data is unstructured, and the value of
full or near-full automation justifies the investment in a more
sophisticated build.

The this thinking discipline for selecting the right point on the
spectrum is the same discipline that governs any organizational
investment decision: match the investment to the validated value. Signal
produced a cost estimate. The Build investment should be proportional to
that estimate. A sprint targeting a \$50,000 annual constraint should
build at the low-to-middle of the spectrum. A sprint targeting a
\$500,000 annual constraint can justify the investment in a high-end
build. The mismatch between sprint investment and sprint value is one of
the most consistent organizational errors in AI deployment:
organizations invest enterprise-level Build budgets in workflow-level
constraints, or invest minimal Build effort in enterprise-level
constraints, and neither produces the expected return.

**8.5 The Build Paths**

The build paths that cover almost every Compound sprint, in order of
increasing complexity and investment.

> 1\. Skills-on-SOPs: a structured prompt or Claude Project trained on
> the documented knowledge from Source. No integrations. No code. The
> agent lives in a conversational interface and the Human Orchestrator
> interacts with it directly for each execution. Appropriate for
> knowledge-intensive workflows where the constraint is the quality of
> the output, not the volume of its production.
>
> 2\. RAG with conversational interface: a knowledge base built from the
> Source documents, connected to a conversational interface. The agent
> retrieves relevant knowledge at runtime and uses it to produce outputs
> that are grounded in the organization\'s actual data. Deployable with
> tools like Notion AI, Claude Projects with uploaded documents, or a
> Airtable base connected to a language model.
>
> 3\. Workflow automation without AI: a trigger-based workflow
> connecting two or three systems without a language model at any step.
> Data moves from one system to another according to defined rules. No
> intelligence required. Zapier, Make, or n8n handle this at low cost
> and low maintenance.
>
> 4\. Agentic workflow: a language model connected to tools and systems,
> taking actions without human prompting at each step. The agent
> receives a trigger, accesses the relevant knowledge and data, produces
> an output, and delivers it to the defined destination. n8n with a
> Claude or OpenAI integration is the most common configuration for
> mid-complexity Compound sprints.
>
> 5\. Custom integration: a bespoke API build connecting systems that do
> not have native connectors, or a multi-agent system with defined agent
> roles and handoffs between them. Requires engineering expertise.
> Appropriate for high-value, high-volume constraints where the
> complexity of the workflow exceeds what low-code tools can handle.

**8.6 Technical Literacy: What You Need to Know**

The Compound Sprint does not require the Human Orchestrator to be a
developer. It does require the Human Orchestrator to be technically
literate enough to evaluate what the Build produces and to make informed
decisions when Build encounters design questions that cannot be resolved
without operational judgment.

Technical literacy in the context of the sprint has a specific meaning.
It does not mean understanding how language models are trained or how
APIs are architected. It means understanding enough about how these
systems work to ask the right questions when reviewing a Build
deliverable and to recognize when a Build decision has organizational
implications that were not addressed in the Design Brief.

**8.6.1 Models and Providers**

The AI models available from major providers are general-purpose
language systems. They do not know your organization. They produce
outputs based on patterns learned from training data combined with the
context provided in the prompt. The organizational implication is that
the quality of the agent\'s output is not primarily a function of which
model is used. It is a function of how well the knowledge from Source
was organized, how clearly the guardrails were written, and how
precisely the task was specified in the prompt architecture.

Model selection matters at the margin. Some models are better at
structured data tasks. Some are better at long-document reasoning. Some
are better at following complex multi-step instructions. The technical
resource advises on model selection based on the task characteristics
identified in the Design Brief. The Human Orchestrator validates that
the selected model produces outputs of acceptable quality in the
prototype environment before Build commits to it for production.

**8.6.2 Tokens and Pricing**

Every interaction with an AI model consumes tokens, approximately
three-quarters of a word each. Every prompt the agent sends and every
response it receives adds to the token count for that interaction.
Providers charge per token, and for high-volume production workflows,
token costs are a line item that belongs in the sprint budget alongside
the Build investment.

The organizational implication of token pricing is that prompt
architecture has a cost dimension in addition to a quality dimension. A
prompt that produces excellent outputs but requires three thousand
tokens to do so costs more than a prompt that produces equally excellent
outputs in eight hundred tokens. The technical resource is responsible
for optimizing the prompt architecture for both quality and cost. The
Human Orchestrator is responsible for approving the cost estimate for
production operation before Build is declared complete.

**8.6.3 Open Source vs. Proprietary**

Proprietary models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google are hosted on the
provider\'s servers. Your data travels through their infrastructure.
Open source models can be run on your own infrastructure. For most
Compound sprints, proprietary models are the appropriate choice: more
capable, continuously updated, and accessible without infrastructure
investment. Open source becomes the right choice when data privacy
requirements prohibit sending operational data to third-party servers,
or when the volume of the workflow makes the variable cost of
proprietary API calls exceed the fixed cost of hosting an open source
model.

The organizational decision-maker for this choice is not the technical
resource. It is the Human Orchestrator in consultation with whoever in
the organization owns data governance and information security. The
technical resource advises on capability and cost. The organizational
stakeholders advise on data handling requirements. Build begins after
both dimensions are satisfied.

**8.6.4 Making AI Work with Your Data**

RAG connects an agent to the organization\'s specific documents and data
at the moment it needs them, without retraining the model. The
organizational implication is that RAG requires ongoing curation: the
knowledge base that the agent retrieves from needs to be kept current as
the organization\'s policies, products, prices, and procedures change. A
knowledge base that was accurate at Build and has not been updated since
is a knowledge base that produces increasingly stale outputs over time.
The Human Orchestrator\'s maintenance responsibility includes the
knowledge base as a component of the agent team\'s ongoing operational
quality.

Fine-tuning, retraining the model on organizational data to change its
default behavior, is appropriate in specific circumstances: when the
organization needs fundamentally different default behavior across
thousands of interactions, when the domain is highly specialized and the
general-purpose model performs poorly without task-specific training, or
when the volume of use is high enough that the ongoing cost of detailed
context engineering exceeds the one-time cost of fine-tuning. For most
Compound sprints, RAG is faster, cheaper, and more flexible than
fine-tuning and is the correct starting architecture.

**8.6.5 Deployment: Where the Build Runs**

The deployment decision determines where the agent workflow lives and
how it is triggered. The options range from a manual interface where the
Human Orchestrator initiates each execution, through scheduled
automation where the workflow runs on a defined cadence, to
event-triggered automation where the workflow runs in response to a
defined trigger in the operational environment.

The Design Brief specifies the deployment architecture because the
deployment architecture has accountability implications. A manually
triggered workflow requires the Human Orchestrator to be available and
attentive at each execution. A scheduled workflow runs without the Human
Orchestrator\'s active initiation, which requires the monitoring and
exception-handling governance to be more robust. An event-triggered
workflow requires the trigger conditions to be precisely defined,
because the workflow runs whenever the trigger fires, including in
situations the design team did not anticipate when those conditions
appear in production.

**8.7 Agile Concepts for the Rest of Us**

Build borrows several operating disciplines from software development
practice that are relevant regardless of whether the Build requires
code.

Iteration over perfection: ship a working version and improve it based
on real-world operation rather than investing in perfect design before
shipping. The this methodology argument for iteration is that the real
operational environment will always reveal assumptions the design team
made that cannot be validated in the design environment. The faster
those assumptions are tested against reality, the lower the cost of
correcting them. The first version of the workflow will not be the best
version. Build it well enough to test, deploy it, observe it, and
improve it in the next sprint\'s Compound stage.

Backlog management: items that did not make it into V1 go on a
documented backlog, not into the current Build. The backlog is a design
artifact, not a wish list. Every item on the backlog should have a
justification for why it was deferred and a priority level that governs
when it will be addressed. The Human Orchestrator owns the backlog. The
Design Team reviews it at each Compound stage.

Definition of done: every sprint Build should have a written definition
of what \'done\' means before Build begins. Not a feel for when the
workflow seems to be working. A specific set of conditions that must be
true before Build can be declared complete. The done test in Section
8.12 is the Compound Sprint\'s definition of done.

**8.8 The Two Instruments: Build Spec Writer and Guardrails Checklist**

Two instruments from the Compound Vault support the Build stage. The
Build Spec Writer produces a developer-ready specification across eight
sections. The Guardrails Checklist ensures every governance decision
from the Design Brief is implemented in the Build before Deliver begins.
Both are produced by the Design Team before Build begins and handed to
the Build team as the authoritative requirements documents.

The Build Spec covers eight sections: workflow summary, inputs (what the
workflow receives and in what form), outputs (what the workflow produces
and where it delivers it), integrations (every system the workflow
touches and the API or connection pattern for each), agent team design
(the specific agents in the workflow and the task each agent performs),
prompt architecture (the instructions provided to each agent at
runtime), guardrails (the five guardrail decisions from the Design Brief
implemented as specific technical constraints), and acceptance criteria
(the specific outputs and behaviors the workflow must produce to pass
the Build done test).

The organizational design value of the Build Spec is that it closes the
translation gap between what the Design Team intended and what the Build
team constructs. A Build Spec written at the level of specificity that
an engineer who was not in any Design session can execute without asking
questions is a Build Spec that eliminates the most expensive source of
Build errors: the undisclosed assumption. Every undisclosed assumption
in a Build Spec is a decision that will be made by the Build team based
on their interpretation of the Design intent. Those interpretations are
sometimes correct. When they are not, the correction cost is paid in
Deliver, not in Build.

**8.9 The Implementation Mistakes Checklist**

Before declaring Build complete, check for five common implementation
errors that are visible from the Build output but are expensive to
correct after Deliver begins.

> 1\. The agent is being used as a chatbot rather than an agent. If the
> Human Orchestrator is manually typing prompts for every execution
> rather than triggering a workflow that runs autonomously, the Build
> has produced a conversational interface, not an agent workflow. The
> test: can the workflow run to completion without the Human
> Orchestrator typing a single character after it is triggered?
>
> 2\. The workflow depends on undocumented knowledge. If the agent\'s
> accuracy in production will depend on information that is not in the
> knowledge layer and cannot be retrieved programmatically, that
> knowledge will drift as the operational environment changes. The Build
> is stable today and degrading tomorrow. The test: could the workflow
> be handed to someone who has never seen the constraint before and
> produce correct outputs?
>
> 3\. The guardrails exist in the Design Brief but not in the deployed
> system. Guardrails that are written down but not built in are not
> guardrails. They are aspirations. The test: trigger each of the five
> guardrail conditions deliberately in the test environment and observe
> whether the workflow responds as the guardrail specifies.
>
> 4\. The Human Orchestrator cannot run the workflow without the builder
> present. If the person who built the system is the only person who can
> operate it, the sprint has not transferred to the organization. The
> workflow will degrade or stop when the builder is unavailable. The
> test: hand the workflow to the Human Orchestrator and have them run it
> from start to finish without assistance. If they cannot, the Build
> needs additional documentation or simplification before it is declared
> done.
>
> 5\. The operational log is not in place. A workflow that runs without
> a log produces outputs that cannot be reviewed, quality trends that
> cannot be detected, and sprint measurement data that cannot be
> produced for Deliver. The log is not an afterthought. It is part of
> the Build, and it must be live and owned by the Human Orchestrator
> before the first production input is processed.

**8.10 The Guardrails Checklist**

The five guardrail questions from Chapter 6 become the five guardrail
implementation checks in Build. For each one, the Build team confirms
that the technical constraint is in place in the deployed system, not
just in the Design Brief.

Data access: the agent can reach the systems it needs and cannot reach
the systems it should not. The access boundaries are enforced at the
infrastructure level, not by a note in the prompt that says \'do not
access payment information.\' Action boundaries: the workflow cannot
take actions that were designated as requiring human approval without
triggering the human approval step. The approval step is built in, not
assumed. Escalation paths: the workflow\'s response to out-of-scope
inputs routes to the correct human in the correct timeframe. The routing
is built and tested in the test environment before production. Quality
monitoring: the operational log captures the four log categories
(unplanned intervention, data gap, edge case, user confusion) in a form
that the Human Orchestrator can review and that the Deliver measurement
can use. Kill switch: the condition that triggers an immediate halt to
the workflow is defined, testable, and actioned by someone with the
authority and availability to act on it when it fires.

**8.11 Sprint: The Three-Week Quoting Workflow**

Meridian\'s Build ran over three weeks. Week one: knowledge base
construction and API connections. Dave\'s estimation tables and Elena\'s
pricing spreadsheet were cleaned and structured for RAG. The CRM
connection was established. The material database connection was
established. No agent was deployed in week one. The knowledge layer was
built first, following the principle that the agent can only be as good
as the knowledge it can access.

Week two: agent workflow construction. The three agents (RFQ Parser,
Material Estimator, Quote Assembler) were built in n8n with Claude
integrations. Each agent was tested individually against ten
representative RFQs from the prior quarter, including two edge cases
from the escalation categories Elena had documented in Source. The
individual agent tests produced acceptable results. The workflow
integration test, running all three agents in sequence against a full
RFQ, revealed one design gap: the output format the Quote Assembler
produced did not match the template format Elena used for customer
delivery. The gap was surfaced to the Design Team, the template was
documented in the Build Spec, and the Quote Assembler was updated.

Week three: integration testing, Human Orchestrator handoff, and
operational log setup. Elena ran the workflow independently against ten
new RFQs under the builder\'s observation. She identified two additional
edge cases that the test environment had not surfaced. Both were
addressed before production go-live. The operational log was configured,
tested, and the review cadence was confirmed: Elena reviews every Monday
morning. Build declared done on day nineteen. Deliver begins on day
twenty.

**8.12 What Done Looks Like**

A Build is done when it passes five questions, each checked against real
operational data from the production environment.

> 1\. Does the workflow produce correct outputs for the standard cases?
> Take ten representative inputs from the prior quarter and run them
> through the deployed workflow. The output for each should match the
> output that the human would have produced, within the quality standard
> specified in the Design Brief\'s success criteria.
>
> 2\. Does the workflow correctly identify and escalate the edge cases?
> Take five edge cases from the escalation categories documented in
> Source and run them through the deployed workflow. Each should trigger
> the escalation protocol specified in the guardrails, routing to the
> correct human within the specified timeframe.
>
> 3\. Can the Human Orchestrator run the full workflow without the
> builder present? The Human Orchestrator runs the workflow from trigger
> to output for three consecutive inputs. The builder observes but does
> not assist. If the Human Orchestrator requires assistance at any
> point, that dependency is a Build gap, not a Human Orchestrator
> training gap.
>
> 4\. Can the Human Orchestrator evaluate whether each output is correct
> without specialized technical knowledge? The Human Orchestrator
> reviews three outputs and provides a quality judgment on each. If the
> Human Orchestrator cannot evaluate the output\'s correctness without
> asking the builder to interpret it, the output format is not suitable
> for the AI-Assisted design specified.
>
> 5\. Have the people whose work changes been prepared for the change?
> The Human Orchestrator has briefed every person whose daily workflow
> is affected by the sprint. Each person can answer two questions: what
> do I do differently now, and what does the agent do that I used to do?
> If any affected person cannot answer both questions, Deliver training
> is not complete and Build is not done.

All five yes: Build is done. Hand off to Deliver. Any no: fix what
failed. Run the test again. Build does not advance to Deliver on a
partial pass.

**9. Deliver**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

We flip the switch on a Wednesday. The first RFQ arrives and the Quoting
Agent processes it in four minutes. The draft lands in Elena\'s review
queue. She opens it, checks the material estimates against her knowledge
of the account, adjusts one line item, and sends. Fourteen minutes
total. It would have taken three hours.

She does not celebrate. She closes the tab and moves to the next item on
her list. That response, quiet, unsurprised, just moving forward, is
actually the success signal we have been waiting for. Elena was
skeptical at the start. She resisted the full automation proposal. She
negotiated for AI-Assisted because she was not ready to trust a system
she had not yet seen work. And now it has worked, and it is just part of
how Tuesday operates.

That is Deliver done right. The technology deployed. The human adapted.
The change held. The workflow is no longer a project. It is
infrastructure.

Getting there required more than flipping a switch. Deployed is not the
same as delivered.

**9.1 Deployed Is Not the Same as Delivered**

Most organizations treat the go-live date as the finish line. The
workflow is live in production. The sprint is complete. Onto the next
initiative. That framing produces the most common sprint failure mode: a
technically functional system that the team quietly stops using within
ninety days because nobody managed the human transition that the
technology required.

Deployment is a technical event. Delivery is an organizational event.
Deployment means the workflow runs in the production environment with
real data. Delivery means the people whose work changed are using the
new workflow as their default, the measurement baseline is in place, and
the organization can prove the sprint produced the return Signal said it
would. Those are different milestones. Deployment happens first.
Delivery takes the week after.

The sprint outcome measurement has two components, and both must be
present for the sprint to be considered delivered. The operational
result answers the question: did the cost identified in Signal decrease,
and by how much? The answer comes from the measurement data, not from a
sense that things feel better. Elena\'s quoting time was fifteen hours
per week. It is now four. The workflow is producing correct draft quotes
for ninety-seven percent of standard RFQs. The three percent edge case
rate matches the design specification. Those numbers confirm the
operational result.

The adoption rate answers the second question: what percentage of the
affected team is using the new workflow as designed rather than routing
around it? The most technically excellent workflow in the world fails
organizationally if the people who are supposed to use it have found
workarounds that let them keep doing the old thing. Adoption rate is not
a soft metric. It is the organizational complement to the operational
result, and together they tell the full story of whether the sprint
worked.

The distinction between Pattern Drag and genuine adoption is important
here. Pattern Drag is the initial friction that appears when new habits
compete with old ones: the person who checks the agent\'s output twice,
the person who calls Elena directly out of reflex before remembering
that the queue exists, the process owner who reverts to manual work when
the first edge case appears. Some Pattern Drag is normal and resolves on
its own as the new workflow becomes familiar. Persistent Pattern Drag,
still present after three or four weeks of live operation, is a design
signal: something in the workflow is harder to use than the old
approach, and the team is routing around it because the old way is
functionally easier. That signal belongs in the sprint retrospective,
not in a change management intervention.

**9.2 Training the People Whose Work Changes**

The most consistent finding across every workflow deployment either of
us has managed is this: train the boundary, not the tool. Most
technology training focuses on how to use the new system: here is the
interface, here are the buttons, here is what the output looks like.
That training produces people who know how to navigate the tool. It does
not produce people who know how to work with it.

Working with an agent team requires a different kind of knowledge. Not
what the interface looks like but where the human\'s work ends and the
agent\'s begins. The handoff. The moment at which the human either
completes an action that enables the agent to proceed, or the agent
completes an output that the human must evaluate before the workflow
continues. The handoff is the point of highest failure risk in any
human-AI workflow, because it is where the human\'s decision about how
to proceed determines whether the agent\'s work compound on good inputs
or propagates errors from bad ones.

At Meridian, the training for the quoting sprint was organized entirely
around the handoffs. Elena\'s training focused on one question: what
does a draft quote that is ready to send look like, and what does one
that needs correction look like? Not how does the interface work. How do
you evaluate what comes out of it? Ty\'s training focused on one
question: what triggers an RFQ routing to Elena\'s exception queue
rather than processing through the standard workflow, and how do you
handle the customer relationship during the one-to-two business day
exception handling window? Dave\'s training focused on one question:
what estimation categories are now in the pricing database versus which
categories still require my judgment, and how do I flag the ones that
require my input before the agent can produce a reliable estimate?

Three people. Three handoffs. Three focused training conversations. The
system training took fifteen minutes per person because the handoffs
were documented. The judgment training took longer because it required
the humans to develop a sense of what good agent output looks like,
which only comes from reviewing enough examples to build pattern
recognition. That pattern recognition is not something you can document
and hand to someone. It develops through use, with access to feedback
when the pattern recognition is wrong.

The implication for Deliver is that training has a duration component
that the go-live date does not accommodate. The Human Orchestrator needs
to be available and attentive in the first two weeks after go-live in a
way that is different from their normal operational cadence. Not as a
babysitter for the system but as the person who is close enough to the
initial operation to see when the pattern recognition is not developing
correctly and to provide the feedback that develops it.

**9.3 The Deploy Readiness Audit**

Before the workflow goes live, six conditions must be true. These are
not aspirational. They are the conditions under which Deliver can
produce a measurable result. A workflow that goes live without meeting
these conditions will produce adoption data that cannot be attributed to
the design and measurement data that cannot be compared to Signal\'s
baseline.

Every integration is connected and tested with real data, not sample
data. The gap between sample data and production data is almost always
larger than the Build team expects. Sample data is clean,
representative, and doesn\'t contain the weird edge cases that appear in
three percent of real transactions but never in a curated test set. The
Deliver readiness audit requires that the workflow has been tested
against real production data from the prior quarter, not against a
representative sample constructed for testing purposes.

The guardrails are active and have been tested against the edge cases
identified in Source. Not just configured. Tested. Each guardrail
condition was deliberately triggered in the test environment and the
workflow responded as the guardrail specified. The team knows the
guardrails work because they watched them fire, not because they read
the Design Brief and assumed they were implemented correctly.

The Human Orchestrator can run the full workflow without the builder
present. This test is non-negotiable. The workflow that requires the
builder to be available for questions is not ready for production. The
Human Orchestrator runs a complete cycle, start to finish, including a
deliberate edge case that requires the escalation path, and manages it
without asking for help. That independence is Deliver\'s done condition
on the human side.

Every person whose daily work changes has been trained and confirmed
ready. Not trained. Trained and confirmed. The difference is a check-in:
have each affected person walk you through what they will do differently
starting Monday. If they can describe it accurately, they are ready. If
they hesitate or describe the old workflow, the training is incomplete
and the go-live date moves.

The operational log is set up, owned by a named person, and has been
reviewed at least once with real data. The log captures unplanned
interventions, data gaps, edge cases, and user confusion. The Human
Orchestrator has reviewed the log format, understands what each category
captures, and has confirmed the review cadence: weekly, Monday morning,
fifteen minutes.

The measurement baseline is documented from Signal and accessible for
comparison. Signal produced a cost estimate. That estimate is the
baseline against which Deliver will measure. Before go-live, the
specific metrics (quoting hours per week, quote turnaround time,
RFQ-to-quote conversion rate) are documented in their current state so
the post-sprint measurement has a clean comparison point.

**9.4 The Per-Role Runbook**

The per-role runbook is the document that makes training stickable. Not
a system manual. Not a comprehensive procedure guide. A one-page
reference document for the specific person whose work changed,
describing their new role in the workflow in plain language they can
consult on day three when the training has faded and a situation arises
that they are not sure how to handle.

Four questions per role. What does this person do differently than
before the sprint? The answer should be specific enough to act on:
\'Before the sprint, Elena processed each RFQ directly from email. After
the sprint, Elena opens the review queue each morning and reviews the
agent-generated drafts, approving standard quotes and routing edge cases
to the manual processing track.\' What does the agent team handle that
this person used to handle? Specific, concrete: \'The agent now performs
the initial RFQ parsing, material pricing lookup, labor hours
calculation, and quote template assembly. Elena previously performed all
of these steps manually for each RFQ.\' How does this person review
agent output? What are they actually looking at, and what are the two or
three things they are specifically checking? What triggers an
escalation, and where does the escalation go?

The runbook lives in the place where the person will actually find it
when they need it. Not in a folder someone created for the sprint. In
the workflow system itself, if possible, so that when the person opens
the queue they also see the reference document. The measure of a good
runbook is whether someone who has been on vacation for two weeks can
use it to orient themselves to the workflow without calling anyone.

**9.5 What Gets Logged**

The operational log is the sprint\'s ongoing intelligence system. It
captures four categories of events that collectively tell the story of
how the workflow is performing and where the next design improvement
should come from.

Unplanned interventions are the times a human had to step in where the
workflow was supposed to operate autonomously. Elena manually processing
an RFQ that should have been handled by the standard agent workflow. Ty
following up with a customer about a quote status that the workflow was
supposed to update automatically. Each unplanned intervention is a
signal: either the edge case is one the design anticipated and the
guardrail is working correctly by routing it to a human, or the edge
case is one the design did not anticipate and the guardrail boundary
needs to be updated.

Data gaps are the times the workflow needed information that does not
exist in accessible form. The agent queried the material database and
found a part number that is not in the system. The RAG knowledge base
returned no relevant results for a customer-specific pricing exception
category. Each data gap is a Source gap: information that should have
been captured in the knowledge layer and was not. The data gap log
drives the Source maintenance work that keeps the knowledge layer
current between sprints.

Edge cases are inputs the design did not account for. The RFQ with
specifications that fall outside all three of the standard material
categories. The customer with a pricing agreement structure that does
not fit any of the documented exception protocols. Edge cases are not
failures. They are the design\'s honest acknowledgment that the world is
more varied than any specification can fully anticipate. The edge case
log is the primary input to the one design change that the Compound
stage retrospective will install before the next sprint.

User confusion events are the times a person whose work changed did not
know what to do. Ty sending an RFQ directly to Elena instead of routing
it through the queue because he forgot the new process. Dave providing
his labor estimates in the old format instead of the new structured
input the workflow requires. User confusion is a training signal, not a
technology signal. The workflow is working. The human is not yet fluent
with the new process. The distinction matters because the response is
different: a technology failure requires a design change, while user
confusion requires a follow-up training conversation and a runbook
improvement.

The log is reviewed weekly by the Human Orchestrator. The review takes
fifteen minutes. The outcome is one of three things: the workflow is
performing within expected parameters, in which case the review closes
with no action. An edge case or data gap has appeared that warrants a
design note for the upcoming retrospective. A pattern of unplanned
interventions or user confusion suggests that a training conversation or
a guardrail adjustment should happen before the next retrospective.

**9.6 The Delivery Test**

Before calling the sprint complete, the Human Orchestrator runs the
delivery test. This is a checklist, not a conversation. Each item either
passes or fails. Nothing proceeds until all items pass.

The Human Orchestrator can run the workflow independently. The log has
at least two weeks of entries and has been reviewed at least twice. The
leadership team has seen the measurement results and confirmed that the
post-sprint numbers can be compared to Signal\'s baseline. The adoption
rate, the percentage of affected roles using the new workflow as
designed, is above eighty percent. The pattern of operational log
entries is consistent with the Design Brief\'s intended performance:
edge case rates are within the specified range, unplanned interventions
are decreasing over time rather than increasing, and user confusion
events have stopped appearing in the last week of the measurement
period.

If all items pass: the sprint is delivered. The workflow moves from
Deliver to Compound. If any item fails: the item identifies what needs
to change. A training issue is addressed with the specific person. A
data gap is addressed with a Source update. An edge case is documented
for the retrospective\'s one design change. A low adoption rate triggers
a conversation with each underperforming role to identify whether the
issue is training, workflow friction, or an unaddressed design problem.

**9.7 The Change Is Not the Launch. The Change Is the Week.**

The most important thing the Human Orchestrator does in Deliver is stay
close to the workflow in the first week after go-live. Not managing it.
Observing it. Watching how the team actually uses the new process in
real conditions, not in the training environment.

The first week after go-live is when the assumptions that survived the
Build process meet the reality they were designed to address. The edge
cases that appeared in one percent of test cases appear in three percent
of production cases because the test sample was not representative. The
user who seemed comfortable in training reaches for the old process the
first time the new one produces an unexpected output. The knowledge
layer that was comprehensive in the test environment has a gap that only
surfaces when the third-quarter pricing updates are not yet reflected.

None of those are failures. All of them are information. The Human
Orchestrator who is close enough to the workflow in the first week to
see them and log them is the Human Orchestrator who arrives at the
Compound retrospective with specific, actionable design improvements
rather than a general sense that things could be better.

The change is not the launch. The launch is the day the switch is
flipped. The change is the week that follows: the week in which the team
proves to itself that the new way of working is viable, that the system
can handle what their operations actually produce, and that the Human
Orchestrator can manage what the system produces. When that week
completes successfully, the sprint is delivered. The organization has
changed.

**9.8 End of the Sprint as a Project. Start of It as Infrastructure.**

When the Delivery Test passes, something important happens to the
sprint. It stops being a project and becomes infrastructure. This
transition is not metaphorical. It has real implications for how the
organization thinks about the sprint from this point forward.

As a project, the sprint had a team, a budget, a timeline, and a defined
scope. Someone was responsible for it. Meetings were scheduled. Progress
was tracked. As infrastructure, the sprint is just how the work runs.
Nobody is managing it as a project. The Human Orchestrator owns it as a
function. The operational log runs. The weekly review happens. The
workflow executes.

The infrastructure frame has one critical implication: it does not run
itself. Infrastructure requires maintenance, and the maintenance of a
human-AI workflow is more active than the maintenance of a piece of
software. The knowledge layer ages as the organization evolves. The
guardrails that were well-calibrated to the edge case rate in Q3 may be
over- or under-restrictive in Q4 if the product mix or customer base
changes. The Human Orchestrator who is performing the right oversight
will catch those changes before they become operational problems. The
Human Orchestrator who is treating the workflow as set-and-forget will
discover them when they surface as sprint failures in the measurement
data.

The Compound stage is where the infrastructure maintenance is
formalized: the retrospective, the one design change, the updated
knowledge map. But the attitude toward maintenance begins at the moment
the Delivery Test passes. The sprint is not finished. It is established.
The work from here is not completion. It is development.

**10. Compound**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Then something changed.

The workflow ran on Tuesday. It ran on Wednesday and Thursday and
Friday. By the end of the second week, Elena had stopped checking it at
the end of every day and started checking it once in the morning. By the
third week, she had stopped checking it and started trusting it. Not
blindly: she still reviewed every draft quote before it left the
building. But the posture changed from supervision to oversight. From
watching to managing.

That posture shift is the organizational signal that Deliver has
completed and Compound has begun. The sprint is no longer a project
being monitored. It is infrastructure being operated. And the job
changes: from building something that works to developing something that
works better.

Compound is the sixth stage of the Sequence, and its name is
intentional. The stage produces the compounding returns that give the
whole framework its economic logic. A sprint that ends at Deliver
produces one return: the cost reduction identified in Signal, measured
in Deliver. A sprint that runs through Compound produces a second
return: the infrastructure improvement that makes the next sprint
cheaper and faster. Six sprints in, the organization is not just running
better. It is accumulating operational capital that reduces the cost of
every subsequent sprint while increasing the rate at which constraints
can be addressed.

**10.1 What Compound Produces**

The Compound stage produces four things, in sequence. The Sprint
Retrospective captures what the sprint produced, what it missed, and
what the design would do differently. The one design change installs the
single most valuable improvement before the next sprint begins. The
Constraint Re-rank updates the Signal Backlog with the new operational
picture the sprint created. The living document updates carry the
sprint\'s permanent outputs, the expanded HAC, the updated Knowledge
Map, the new Hybrid Org Today, into the organizational record that the
next sprint will build on.

Each of these four outputs has compounding value. The Sprint
Retrospective\'s learnings reduce the cost of the next sprint\'s Design
phase. The one design change extends the capability of the deployed
workflow, increasing its return in future quarters without requiring a
new sprint. The re-ranked Signal Backlog ensures the next sprint
addresses the highest-value constraint in the post-sprint operational
picture rather than the highest-value constraint in the pre-sprint
picture. The living document updates give the next sprint\'s Source
phase a head start: the Knowledge Map already exists, the HAC governance
framework is already established, and the most expensive sprint work,
the work that is done from scratch in sprint one, is already done.

**10.2 The Sprint Retrospective**

The Sprint Retrospective is a structured working session, not a debrief.
The difference is the output: a debrief produces a shared understanding
of what happened. A working session produces a decision: the one design
change that will be installed before the next sprint begins. Without
that decision, the retrospective has not completed. It has just
reviewed.

Three lenses structure the retrospective. Pattern Drag identifies where
old habits resisted the new design. Which team members routed around the
agent workflow? Which supervisors approved outputs without reviewing
them because they did not have the time or the confidence to evaluate
them? Which process owners reverted to manual work when the first
unfamiliar output appeared? Every Pattern Drag instance is a design
improvement opportunity: the boundary between what the agent handles and
what the human handles was not clear enough, or the human training did
not develop the judgment needed to evaluate the output, or the
governance framework did not make the escalation path obvious enough to
use under pressure.

Pattern Gap identifies the difference between the designed workflow and
the workflow that actually ran in production. Some gaps are expected:
the design made assumptions that the first sprint\'s operational data
corrects. Some gaps indicate that the training did not produce the
behavior the design required. Some gaps indicate that the guardrails
were not calibrated correctly for the actual distribution of inputs.
Each gap category has a different remedy, and the retrospective\'s job
is to identify which category each gap falls into before deciding what
design change to make.

Pattern Lift measures the organizational return on the elevated work
that the sprint was supposed to create. Elena recovered eleven hours per
week from the quoting sprint. Where did those eleven hours go? If they
went into the strategic operations work that had been backlogged for two
years, the Pattern Lift is real and the sprint produced its full
organizational return. If those eleven hours filled with additional
quoting volume because the business grew, the operational result is
still valid but the organizational elevation did not happen.
Understanding the difference matters for workforce planning: if the
recovered hours immediately refill with the same work, the sprint has
produced efficiency without elevation, and the next sprint\'s design
should explicitly include a plan for what the elevated human work will
be.

**10.3 Measuring What Compounded**

The Compounding Scorecard tracks what each sprint produced. For each
sprint: the constraint addressed, the Signal cost estimate, the Deliver
measurement result, the delta, and the cumulative impact across all
sprints to date. The scorecard is updated at the Compound stage and
reviewed at the quarterly operating session.

The number that matters most on the scorecard is not any single
sprint\'s delta. It is the trajectory of the deltas over time relative
to the investment per sprint. If the deltas are growing and the cost per
sprint is declining, the Compound Sequence is working as designed: the
infrastructure accumulated in each sprint is compounding the return of
each subsequent sprint. If the deltas are flat or the cost per sprint is
not declining, the Compound stage is not producing its full return: the
living documents are not being maintained, the one design change is not
being installed, or the Signal process is selecting surface constraints
rather than structural ones.

The productivity ratio, delta divided by sprint cost, should improve
over time. Sprint one at a \$412K delta and a four-week Build investment
represents a certain productivity ratio. Sprint three, at a similar
delta achieved in a two-week Build investment using the infrastructure
sprint one created, represents a significantly better productivity
ratio. That improvement is the compounding return the framework is
designed to produce, and the Compounding Scorecard is the instrument
that makes it visible.

**10.4 The One Design Change Rule**

Every Compound stage installs exactly one design change before the next
sprint begins. The discipline of one is not a limitation on ambition. It
is a structural protection against the organizational failure mode that
makes most improvement programs ineffective.

The failure mode is familiar. The retrospective produces twelve
insights. The team writes them all down. The list becomes the backlog.
The backlog accumulates. Nobody owns it. The next sprint begins with the
same design that produced the twelve insights, because none of them were
prioritized, assigned, and verified as installed. Three sprints later,
the team is writing the same twelve insights again, slightly rephrased,
because the underlying design never changed.

One change, made and verified before the next sprint begins, breaks that
cycle. It does not matter how small the change is. What matters is that
it is specific, assigned to a named person, implemented in the actual
deployed workflow (not in a design document), and verified as working
before the next sprint\'s Signal conversation begins. The retrospective
that produces one verified change is more valuable than the
retrospective that produces ten insights. Insights do not compound.
Changes do.

The process for selecting the one design change has a specific order.
First, list all the improvement candidates that emerged from the three
retrospective lenses. Second, filter for changes that are within the
Human Orchestrator\'s authority and capability to implement without
requiring a new sprint. Third, rank the remaining candidates by expected
impact on two dimensions: the change that most reduces the exception
rate and the change that most extends the range of cases the agent
handles autonomously. The intersection of those two dimensions
identifies the candidate that produces the most compounding return per
unit of implementation effort. That is the one design change.

**10.5 Constraint Re-rank**

Every sprint changes the organizational landscape. The constraint the
sprint addressed has been reduced. The organizational capacity freed by
the sprint has been redirected to higher-leverage work. The sprint\'s
operation has surfaced adjacent constraints that were not visible before
the workflow ran in production. And the sprint\'s success has changed
the leadership team\'s confidence in what the Sprint program can
address, which typically surfaces new candidate constraints that were
previously considered too complex or too risky to sprint on.

The re-rank happens with the same Signal discipline that produced the
original constraint statement. Not by gut feel. Not by whoever argues
most persuasively. By applying the four constraint questions: Is it
within our control? What does it cost? How long has it existed? Is there
a Human Orchestrator available? The re-ranked backlog reflects the
post-sprint operational reality, not the pre-sprint picture. And it is
the input to the next quarterly operating session, where the next sprint
is committed.

The re-rank also performs a function that the retrospective alone
cannot: it ensures the sprint program is always working on the
highest-value constraint in the current state of the organization, not
the highest-value constraint in a static picture of the organization
that was taken at the start of the program. Organizations that do not
re-rank after each sprint gradually drift toward solving constraints
that were once high-value but have been superseded by the operational
changes their earlier sprints produced.

**10.6 Update the Living Documents**

The Compound stage updates three documents that carry the sprint\'s
permanent contributions to the organizational record. The Hybrid
Accountability Chart gains the new entries that the sprint created:
every agent team deployed in the sprint is now a permanent HAC entry
with a named supervisor, an autonomy level, and a governance framework.
The document that used to show the organization\'s human accountability
structure now shows a richer picture: the human accountability structure
plus the agent team layer that runs alongside it.

The Knowledge Map is updated with everything Source and Deliver
discovered. The gaps that existed before the sprint, documented as
Missing sources, either have been filled (and move to Clean status) or
remain as ongoing gaps (with updated capture plans). The new knowledge
that was generated during the sprint, the exception protocols that were
documented, the estimation tables that were structured, the
customer-specific pricing categories that were codified, all of it is
added to the Knowledge Map. The next sprint\'s Source phase starts with
this map already drawn. The depth of work required to complete Source in
sprint two is a fraction of what sprint one required.

The Hybrid Org Today is updated to reflect the post-sprint state: the
active sprint status (Compound stage complete, sprint closed), the
re-ranked Signal Backlog, the current HAC entry count (grown by whatever
the sprint added), and the Compounding Scorecard with the sprint\'s
results recorded. The Hybrid Org Today is the one-page answer to the
question every CEO and every leadership team should be able to answer on
demand: what is the current state of our Co-Intelligent Co-operation
program? The answer is on one page, updated quarterly, and can be handed
to a board member or a new executive without a briefing.

**10.7 Infrastructure Compounds**

The reason sprint six is faster and cheaper than sprint one is not that
the team has become more skilled at running sprints, though they have.
It is that every sprint leaves behind infrastructure that the next
sprint builds on top of.

By sprint six at Jesse\'s company, the Knowledge Map that Source would
have taken three weeks to build from scratch in sprint one was already
eighty percent complete. The Source session for sprint six took
forty-five minutes. The Design decisions that would have required two
full days of working sessions in sprint one were made in three hours,
because the HAC governance framework from the prior five sprints had
already established the decision templates: the standard for AI-Assisted
designation, the escalation trigger format, the kill switch conditions.
The Build phase that would have required three weeks of custom
development in sprint one was completed in four days, because the
integration patterns, the prompt architecture templates, and the
deployment configurations were already documented from prior builds.

That velocity improvement is not a bonus. It is the compounding return
the framework is designed to produce. The organization that ran six
sprints over eighteen months is not eighteen months ahead of an
organization that ran one. It is three to four years ahead in
operational capability, because the infrastructure those six sprints
created compresses the development timeline for every subsequent sprint.

**10.7.1 The 45 Minutes That Saved Weeks**

The most concrete illustration of this infrastructure effect happened in
the third sprint at Jesse\'s company. The sprint targeted the client
onboarding workflow, a process that sat downstream of the project
coordination function the second sprint had addressed. Source was
scheduled for three weeks based on the time sprint one\'s Source had
required.

On day one of Source, the team opened the Knowledge Map from sprint two.
It contained complete documentation of the project management system\'s
data structure, the CRM connection patterns, and the client
communication workflow, because the project coordination sprint had
mapped all of those in its own Source phase. The onboarding workflow
shared sixty percent of the same underlying knowledge architecture. The
Source session that was scheduled for three weeks closed in forty-five
minutes. The team spent the remaining time on the thirty percent of the
knowledge map that was genuinely new: the client-specific onboarding
protocols that the project coordination sprint had not needed to
capture.

Sprint three ran in six weeks. Sprint one had run in fourteen. The
velocity difference was not efficiency. It was infrastructure. The
forty-five-minute Source session was only possible because two prior
sprints had done the knowledge work that would otherwise have had to be
done from scratch.

**10.8 Compound Is Not the Same as Scale**

Every vendor uses scale to describe what happens when their product
reaches a certain volume. Scale is a quantity claim: more users, more
transactions, more output from the same system. It is not a quality
claim, and it is not a design claim.

Compound is a design claim. The organization that is compounding is not
producing more output from the same design. It is producing better
output from an improving design at declining cost per sprint. The
improvement is in the design, not just the volume. Each sprint\'s
retrospective installs a change that makes the next sprint more precise.
Each sprint\'s Source work builds knowledge that makes the next
sprint\'s knowledge work cheaper. Each sprint\'s HAC entry establishes
governance precedents that make the next sprint\'s Design decisions
faster.

A company can scale an AI tool without compounding. It adds more users,
processes more transactions, and produces more output from the same
underlying design. The design never improves because there is no
retrospective, no design change rule, no living documents. Six months
later the tool is doing more of the same thing it was doing on day one,
and the organizational capability of the people using it is unchanged.

A company that compounds is doing something different. It is running a
program that gets smarter every quarter, that produces infrastructure
with each sprint, that develops the organizational capability to address
increasingly complex constraints over time. Scaling is addition.
Compounding is multiplication. The difference shows up on the
Compounding Scorecard by sprint four: the organization that is
compounding shows a productivity ratio that is improving. The
organization that is scaling shows a productivity ratio that is flat.

**11. The Rhythm**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The roster had thirteen people last year. Eight now. The hire that was
supposed to address the project coordination gap was never posted. The
Build addressed it instead. The work that would have required a
full-time employee now runs on an agent team supervised by one person
who spends four hours a week on it instead of forty.

That is sprint one. Sprint two addressed a different constraint: the
knowledge that was concentrated in a senior engineer four years from
retirement. Sprint three addressed the client onboarding workflow. By
sprint six, the organization is not just running better. It is designed
differently. The accountability chart is different. The workflows are
different. The headcount math is different. None of that happened
because one sprint worked. It happened because the Rhythm worked.

The Rhythm is the cadence that turns a sprint into a program. Quarterly.
Against real operational constraints. With a standing governance
structure that makes the next sprint as certain as the current one.
Organizations that run one sprint and then pause produce one result.
Organizations that run the Rhythm produce compounding returns.

**11.1 What Rhythm Means in This Book**

The quarterly cadence is not a preference or a convention. It is the
tempo at which real organizations can absorb, validate, and extend
operational change without overwhelming the team\'s capacity to change
while continuing to run the business.

The organizations that try to sprint faster than quarterly produce
change that does not hold. The sprint\'s Deliver phase requires two to
three weeks of active management by the Human Orchestrator to ensure
adoption is complete. The Compound stage requires its own time: the
retrospective, the design change installation, the knowledge map update,
the Signal re-rank. If a new sprint begins before the previous sprint
has completed its Compound stage, the two sprints are competing for the
same Human Orchestrator\'s attention, and neither gets the quality of
oversight either one requires.

The organizations that sprint slower than quarterly lose the compounding
momentum that makes the program effective. The infrastructure built in
one sprint depreciates if it sits unused: the knowledge layer ages, the
Human Orchestrator\'s oversight habits weaken, and the leadership
team\'s engagement with the sprint program declines because the
quarterly review no longer produces a commitment they can act on in the
current quarter. Quarterly is the right frequency because it matches the
organizational planning rhythm that most well-run businesses already
have: four times per year, leadership steps back from execution to
evaluate and commit. The sprint program installs itself inside that
rhythm rather than creating a competing one.

**11.2 The Quarterly Operating Session**

The quarterly operating session is the governance meeting that ensures
the sprint program continues. It runs once per quarter, typically as a
two-hour addition to the quarterly planning meeting that most
organizations already hold. It has four outputs, and it does not close
until all four are confirmed.

The prior sprint\'s result measured and confirmed. The Human
Orchestrator presents the sprint outcome against Signal\'s baseline: the
cost before, the cost after, the delta, the adoption rate, and the
Pattern Lift. The leadership sponsor confirms that the result is
attributable to the sprint design and that the measurement methodology
is sound. This confirmation is not a formality. It is the organizational
accountability check that ensures the sprint program is producing real
returns rather than optimistic reporting.

The Signal Backlog re-ranked. The leadership team reviews the current
backlog, validates that the rankings reflect the post-sprint operational
reality, and confirms that the top candidate is genuinely the
highest-value constraint in the organization as it currently stands.
This re-rank takes thirty minutes at most when the Compound stage was
completed correctly: the backlog is already updated, the post-sprint
landscape is already documented in the Hybrid Org Today, and the
leadership team is looking at a current picture rather than
reconstructing it in the meeting.

The next sprint committed. One named constraint. One named Human
Orchestrator. One start date. One quarterly review date. The commitment
is public, made in front of the full leadership team, and recorded in
the Sprint Planning Canvas that the Human Orchestrator takes from the
session. An uncommitted quarterly operating session is a quarterly
operating session that failed. The only acceptable outcome of the
session is a specific commitment, not a decision to decide next month.

The Hybrid Accountability Chart updated. Any new HAC entries from the
sprint just completed are confirmed as permanent. The chart now shows
the organization as it actually operates: the human accountability
structure plus the agent team layer that runs alongside it. The updated
chart is distributed to the leadership team before the session closes.

**11.2.1 How It Maps to Your Operating System**

If you run EOS, the quarterly operating session maps directly to your
quarterly offsite. The Signal Backlog is your AI-specific Issues list,
ranked by cost and updated after each sprint. The sprint commitment is
your AI Rock for the next quarter. The Sprint Planning Canvas is the
Rock definition sheet, with the constraint statement as the Rock goal
and the Human Orchestrator as the Rock owner. The Hybrid Org Today lives
in your V/TO as the AI layer of your organizational picture.

If you run Scaling Up, the quarterly session maps to your quarterly
planning meeting. The sprint commitment is a quarterly priority with a
measurable outcome: not \'implement AI in the quoting workflow\' but
\'recover \$250K of the \$558K quoting constraint cost identified in
Signal by reducing Elena\'s quoting time from fifteen hours to four
hours per week.\' The metric is owned by a named person, measured
against a specific baseline, and reported at the next quarterly session.

If you run a less formal planning cadence, the quarterly operating
session creates the structure. The session itself is the planning
rhythm. The Human Orchestrator is the accountability structure. The
Compounding Scorecard is the measurement system. The session gives the
sprint program a governance home inside the organization\'s existing
decision-making structure, whatever form that takes.

**11.3 The Hybrid Org Today**

The Hybrid Org Today is a one-page living document that answers one
question on demand: what does our Co-Intelligent Co-operation program
currently look like? It contains five elements. The active sprint
status: which sprint is running, what stage it is in, and when the next
quarterly review is scheduled. The Signal Backlog: the ranked list of
validated constraints, with cost estimates, waiting for sprint
assignment. The Hybrid Accountability Chart entries: every agent team
currently deployed, with its human supervisor, its autonomy level, and
its last retrospective date. The completed sprint record: a summary row
for each closed sprint, showing the constraint, the Signal estimate, the
Deliver result, and the delta. The next quarterly review date.

The document is updated at each Compound stage and reviewed at each
quarterly operating session. It is never longer than one page, because
its value is legibility: the ability to hand it to any leadership team
member, any board member, or any new executive and have them understand
the program\'s current state without a briefing. A Hybrid Org Today that
requires explanation is a Hybrid Org Today that has not been maintained
at the right level of synthesis.

The documents that feed the Hybrid Org Today, the full HAC, the complete
Knowledge Map, the detailed Sprint Retrospectives, all exist as
supporting documents. The Hybrid Org Today summarizes them. The one-page
discipline forces the Human Orchestrator and the team to synthesize
rather than accumulate, producing organizational clarity about the
program\'s current state rather than a archive of sprint documentation
that nobody reads.

**11.4 The Headcount Paradox, Resolved**

By sprint six, something measurable has happened to the headcount math.
It is not dramatic. It is structural. Revenue and headcount have begun
to decouple.

The decoupling does not happen all at once. It happens at the margin,
sprint by sprint. Sprint one eliminates the need for one hire that would
have been approved in Q3 planning. Sprint two extends the tenure of a
senior engineer by making his knowledge transferable rather than
personal, reducing the retention risk that would have required a
succession hire. Sprint three reduces the client onboarding time from
three weeks to four days, allowing the same account management team to
handle thirty percent more clients without adding headcount. Six sprints
in, the organization can handle significantly more operational volume
than it could at sprint one, with the same team.

This is the practical resolution of the headcount paradox described in
Chapter 1. AI was supposed to change the math of scaling. The tools
alone did not change it. The Compound Sprint program, run on a quarterly
Rhythm against real operational constraints, did change it. Not because
the agent teams are cheaper than humans (though they often are), but
because the design work that each sprint requires forces the
organization to be explicit about what work actually requires human
capacity and what work can be handled by a well-designed system. That
explicitness is what the headcount paradox was missing: not cheaper
workers, but a structured process for deciding which work needs people
at all.

The organizations that resolve the headcount paradox through the
Compound Sprint program do not do it by reducing their teams. They do it
by ensuring that the humans on their teams are doing the work that
genuinely requires them. The retained humans are more engaged, more
effective, and more likely to stay. The work that the agent teams handle
is being done more consistently and at higher volume than the humans
were able to sustain. The organizational output grows. The headcount
does not have to grow with it.

**11.5 The Compounding Scorecard**

The Compounding Scorecard is the P&L of the sprint program. For each
sprint: the constraint addressed, the Signal cost estimate, the Deliver
measurement result, the delta, and the running cumulative impact. It is
updated at each Compound stage and reviewed at each quarterly operating
session.

Two numbers matter most. The delta, the difference between the Signal
estimate and the Deliver result, tells you whether each sprint did what
it was designed to do. A delta that is significantly below the Signal
estimate indicates either a Signal problem (the cost was overstated) or
a Deliver problem (the sprint did not produce the full return). A delta
that matches or exceeds the Signal estimate confirms the sprint\'s
design and validates the methodology for the next sprint\'s commitment.

The cumulative impact, the running sum of all sprint deltas to date,
tells the story of the program. At sprint one, the cumulative impact is
one sprint\'s return. At sprint six, it is the sum of six quarters of
sprints, each building on the infrastructure of the ones before it. The
cumulative impact relative to the total investment across all sprints,
the total Build cost, the Human Orchestrator time, the quarterly
operating session overhead, is the program\'s return on investment. That
number belongs in the board presentation, the strategic plan, and the
CEO\'s answer to the question every investor and every peer will
eventually ask: what is your AI investment actually producing?

**11.6 What Compounding Looks Like, Sprint Six**

A company is six to eight sprints in. Not a concept. A real operational
state that either of us can describe because we have seen it from inside
organizations running the program.

The Hybrid Accountability Chart has between eight and fifteen entries:
eight to fifteen functions where agent teams work alongside humans, each
with a named supervisor, an autonomy level, and a governance framework
that has been updated at least twice through retrospective design
changes. The Knowledge Map covers sixty to seventy percent of the
organization\'s operational knowledge in documented, connected form. The
Signal Backlog is ranked and maintained quarterly, with five to eight
validated candidates at any given time. The Human Orchestrator network
includes three to five people in the organization who have each run at
least one sprint and can run another without external support.

The quarterly operating session takes ninety minutes instead of three
hours because the agenda is familiar and the artifacts are current. The
Signal Backlog review takes fifteen minutes because the backlog is
already ranked and the post-sprint landscape is already documented in
the Hybrid Org Today. The sprint commitment takes thirty minutes because
the leadership team has enough sprint experience to evaluate a
constraint candidate quickly and to predict the sprint\'s likely shape
before Source even begins.

The Human Orchestrators in the organization have developed a shared
vocabulary and a shared set of expectations. They know what a
well-formed constraint statement looks like, what a complete Knowledge
Map requires, and what a Design Brief needs to contain to pass the
Design Gate without revision. That shared vocabulary reduces the
coordination cost of every sprint and eliminates the re-education
overhead that slows sprint one down.

**11.6.1 Two Business Lines, Half the Staff**

At Jesse\'s company, sprint six produced the organizational picture that
the program had been building toward across eighteen months. Two
business lines were running at higher volume than before sprint one. The
team was eight people instead of thirteen. Billings per person were
higher. Profitability was higher.

The five roles that were not filled or not backfilled were not missed.
The work they would have done was absorbed by designed systems built
sprint by sprint across six quarters. The project coordination function,
the client communication drafting, the weekly reporting, the onboarding
workflow, and the knowledge capture system that had been a person\'s
primary job: all of it running on agent teams supervised by the Human
Orchestrators who had been elevated from the task-level work those agent
teams now handled.

The headcount did not shrink by design. It shrank by default: the sprint
program made the tasks that would have justified those roles unnecessary
as standalone human responsibilities. When the roles turned over
naturally, through departure or internal movement, they were not
backfilled because the sprint program had already built the alternative.

**11.6.2 Three Sprints: Meridian Manufacturing**

Meridian ran three sprints in year one. Each one was faster than the
last and produced a larger efficiency gain per week of Build time
invested.

Sprint one, the quoting bottleneck: fourteen weeks from Signal to
Compound. \$558K Signal estimate, \$412K recovered in year one, delta of
\$412K. Sprint two, the knowledge-at-risk constraint around Dave\'s
retirement: eight weeks from Signal to Compound. The constraint was less
about direct cost than about organizational resilience, but the
documentation and knowledge transfer program produced an estimated
\$280K in avoided future recruitment and re-training costs. Sprint
three, the scheduling coordination function: five weeks from Signal to
Compound. Two hours per week of manual scheduling coordination, fully
automated, recovering approximately \$45K in VP-of-Operations time
annually.

Compounding Scorecard at year-end: \$737K cumulative delta across three
sprints. Sprint three ran in five weeks. Sprint one ran in fourteen. The
velocity improvement was not the team getting faster. It was the
infrastructure from sprint one and two making sprint three\'s Source,
Design, and Build phases run against an already-built foundation.

**11.6.3 The Company That Was Already Ready**

Not every first sprint is as heavy as Meridian\'s. Some organizations
arrive at their first sprint with most of the infrastructure already in
place: documentation habits already established, operational metrics
already tracked, leadership teams already aligned on the highest-cost
constraint. For those organizations, sprint one runs faster than
expected and produces a result that confirms the program\'s design.

The organizations that are already ready have usually been preparing,
unknowingly, for a long time. They have been running EOS or Scaling Up
for several years and have built the accountability discipline that
Signal and Design depend on. They have a leadership team that has been
measuring operational costs for long enough that the Signal
quantification exercise produces a number in hours rather than days.
They have a Human Orchestrator candidate who is clearly qualified: one
person who knows the constraint workflow well enough to have written a
design document for it, if only someone had asked.

For those organizations, the sprint program feels less like a
transformation and more like a language for something they were already
doing intuitively. The framework makes the intuition explicit, provides
the governance structure for managing the agent teams that the intuition
would have produced anyway, and gives the leadership team a common
vocabulary for the quarterly conversations that will extend the program
sprint by sprint.

**11.7 The Co-Intelligent Company**

Six sprints in, the Co-Intelligent Company is not a future state. It is
the operational reality of an organization that has run the Rhythm. The
accountability chart has been extended to include agent teams. The
workflows have been explicitly designed for human and AI collaboration.
The headcount math has changed. The operational knowledge is documented
and connected. The quarterly cadence produces a new sprint commitment
every ninety days.

That is the company this book exists to help you build. Not a company
that uses AI tools. A company where humans and AI operate as one system,
each contributing what they are best suited to contribute, with explicit
accountability for every output, compounding the result every quarter.

The people in that company are not less important than they were before
the sprint program began. They are more important. The work they do
requires more of what makes them irreplaceable. The decisions they make
carry more organizational weight because the task-level execution that
used to consume their attention has moved to systems built to handle it
reliably. The organization is more dependent on their judgment, their
relationships, and their strategic thinking than it was before the
sprint program began, because those are the capabilities that the agent
teams cannot replicate.

That is the promise of Co-Intelligent Co-operation. Not that AI will run
the company. That AI will run the parts of the company that do not
require a human to run, freeing the humans to run the parts that do.

**12. Managing the Synthetic Workforce**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

We need to talk about something most books on AI avoid.

Deploying AI into your operations is not just a technology project. It
is a workforce event. When the Compound Sprint redesigns a workflow and
installs an agent team, the organization has added workers. Not
metaphorical workers. Workers with specific capabilities, specific
limitations, specific development trajectories, and specific management
requirements. The humans whose work changes are not interacting with
software. They are working alongside teammates who happen to be
synthetic. How those relationships are built, managed, and developed
over time determines whether the sprint compounds or stalls. And almost
nobody is talking about how to manage that.

This chapter exists because the people leader in a Co-Intelligent
Company is not doing the same job as the people leader in a human-only
organization. They are doing a more complex version of the same
fundamental job: ensuring that every member of the workforce, human and
synthetic, is well-positioned to contribute their best work,
well-managed against a clear standard, and well-developed toward greater
capability over time. The tools are different. The principles are the
same.

**12.1 What a Synthetic Workforce Actually Is**

A synthetic workforce is the combination of human workers and AI agent
teams operating together on shared operational work. Not humans using AI
tools. Not AI systems running alongside humans. Humans and agents
working the same problems, with defined accountabilities for each,
inside the same governance structure.

The distinction between tools and workforce is not semantic. It is
organizational. A tool is something a human uses. The human provides the
intelligence, the judgment, the accountability for the outcome. The tool
amplifies the human\'s capacity to execute. A workforce member is an
entity with its own defined accountability: a set of tasks it owns, a
standard it is held to, and a human supervisor responsible for managing
it toward that standard.

When an agent team in the HAC is assigned a set of tasks, given access
to a knowledge layer, given guardrails that define the boundary of its
authority, and given a Human Orchestrator responsible for its
performance, that agent team is a workforce member, not a tool. The
organizational implications of that distinction are the subject of this
chapter.

Most organizations are managing their AI agents as tools: purchased,
deployed, used when convenient, and replaced when a better tool appears.
The organizations that will compound over time will manage their AI
agents as workforce members: developed against a performance standard,
monitored for quality, improved through regular design iterations, and
aligned with the organizational strategy that governs how all work gets
done. The difference between those two approaches is the difference
between an organization that has AI and an organization that is
Co-Intelligent.

**12.2 The People Leader\'s Role in a Synthetic Workforce**

The people leader in a Co-Intelligent Company has a role that most HR
and organizational development frameworks have not yet described. The
traditional people leader manages the human workforce: recruiting,
developing, deploying, and retaining the human talent the organization
needs to execute its strategy. In a Co-Intelligent Company, that scope
expands. The people leader is now responsible for the health and
development of a workforce that includes both human workers and
synthetic agent teams.

This is not a diminishment of the people leader\'s role. It is an
expansion. The same the work design disciplines that produce effective
human workforces, clear accountabilities, strong development systems,
regular performance feedback, alignment between individual work and
organizational strategy, are the disciplines that produce effective
synthetic workforces. The people leader who understands those
disciplines is the person best positioned to apply them to the full
workforce, human and synthetic alike.

The people leader\'s specific responsibilities in managing the synthetic
workforce fall into four areas. First: workforce design. The people
leader is the organizational owner of the question of how work should be
distributed across the human and synthetic workforce. This is not a
technology decision. It is an this approach decision, and it belongs to
the people function. Second: performance standards. The people leader
owns the standards against which both human workers and agent teams are
measured. Those standards should be consistent with each other: the
human VP of Operations and the agent team she supervises should both
have visible, measurable performance expectations. Third: workforce
development. The people leader tracks how the human and synthetic
workforce is developing over time and ensures that the organization has
a deliberate development strategy for both. Fourth: workforce health.
The people leader monitors the relationship between human workers and
their synthetic teammates, identifying friction, misalignment, and the
organizational symptoms that emerge when that relationship is not
working well.

**12.3 The Human-Agent Relationship at Work**

The relationship between a human worker and an AI agent team is not the
relationship between a manager and a direct report. It is not the
relationship between a user and software. It is something genuinely new,
and organizations that try to manage it through either of those familiar
frames will manage it poorly.

The human-agent relationship in a well-designed Co-Intelligent workflow
is most accurately described as a supervised production relationship.
The human defines the goal, reviews the output, makes the judgment calls
the agent cannot make, and is accountable for the outcome. The agent
executes the defined tasks, produces the outputs, handles the volume,
and flags the exceptions. The quality of that relationship depends on
three factors that the people leader owns.

First: clarity of the division. When the human knows exactly what the
agent is responsible for and what they themselves are responsible for,
the relationship functions smoothly. When the division is ambiguous, the
human either over-supervises (reviewing outputs the agent was designed
to handle autonomously) or under-supervises (assuming the agent is
handling exceptions it was designed to escalate). Both failure modes
reduce the sprint\'s value. The Hybrid Accountability Chart and the Work
Deconstruction are the organizational design instruments for creating
this clarity. The people leader ensures those instruments are in place,
complete, and kept current.

Second: quality of feedback. In a human workforce, performance improves
through feedback: the manager\'s observations about what is working and
what is not, communicated in a way the employee can act on. In a
synthetic workforce, the agent team\'s performance improves through
design iteration: the Human Orchestrator\'s observations about the
workflow\'s edge cases, errors, and improvement opportunities,
communicated through the sprint retrospective and the one design change
rule. The people leader\'s role is to ensure that feedback loops exist
for the synthetic workforce and that the Human Orchestrators in the
organization have the organizational support to use them. That means
protecting the time for retrospectives, providing the facilitation tools
to run them well, and ensuring that the design changes they produce are
implemented before the next sprint begins.

Third: human agency. The most organizationally damaging failure mode in
the human-agent relationship is the human who feels that their role has
been diminished by the agent\'s presence. This feeling is almost always
a design failure: the Work Deconstruction allocated too much of the
interesting work to the agent, leaving the human in a monitoring role
without meaningful judgment engagement. Or the AI-Assisted designation
was implemented in a way that the human experiences as checking outputs
rather than making decisions. Or the human\'s elevated work, the
Management and Leadership layer that Work Deconstruction was supposed to
free them for, was never actually assigned or resourced.

The people leader\'s job is to watch for that feeling and to treat it as
a design signal, not a change management problem. A human who feels
diminished by their agent team is almost always in an organization that
designed the work correctly on paper but did not follow through on the
Pattern Lift that was supposed to result. The agent took the task work.
The higher work was not provided. The human is left with less to do and
no clearer sense of what they are valued for. That is an the structural
framework failure, and it belongs to the people leader to name it and
correct it.

**12.4 Job Descriptions Have Not Changed --- and That Is the Problem**

There is a profound this practice gap sitting at the center of most
companies\' AI efforts, and it is visible in one document: the job
description.

Job descriptions in most organizations have not meaningfully changed in
twenty years. They describe the tasks a role performs, the skills
required to perform them, and the qualifications that indicate a
candidate is likely to have those skills. They are written for human
workers performing human work. They assume that the person in the role
will be doing the tasks listed, manually, using their own judgment and
their own time.

When an organization deploys a Compound Sprint and redesigns the work,
the job description does not change. The tasks in the HAC\'s Human
Judgment Required column are now a subset of what used to be the full
role. The tasks in the Agent-Assisted and Fully Automatable columns are
now performed by the agent team. But the job description still lists all
of them. The hiring process still screens for candidates who can perform
all of them. The performance review still evaluates the person against
all of them. The organizational signal the job description sends is that
the work has not changed, even though it has.

This creates three specific organizational problems. The first is
misaligned hiring. An organization that has run three sprints and
redesigned three roles is no longer hiring for the same work those roles
used to perform. But if the job descriptions have not been updated, the
hiring process will screen for candidates whose skills are concentrated
in the task-level work that the agent teams now handle, rather than in
the Management and Leadership work that the humans should now be doing.
The organization will keep hiring people who are excellent at the work
the organization no longer needs them to do.

The second is misaligned performance evaluation. Performance reviews
that measure a human against the full task list of the pre-sprint job
description will show high performance on the task-level work (because
the agent is doing most of it) and underperform on the Management and
Leadership work (because the organization has not yet made that work
visible and supported it). The performance data will be misleading. The
human may look productive on paper while the organizational value they
were supposed to add through elevated work is not materializing.

The third is misaligned organizational identity. When a person\'s job
description describes work they no longer do, there is a quiet but
significant identity gap. The person knows what the description says.
They know what they actually do. The gap between those two things
produces a form of organizational dissonance that surfaces as
disengagement, confusion about career trajectory, or resentment toward
the AI systems that changed their work without the organization formally
acknowledging the change.

**12.5 Redesigning Job Descriptions for Synthetic Teams**

The job description for a role in a Co-Intelligent Company needs to
describe the work the human actually does inside the synthetic team, not
the work they would do if no agent team existed. That is a fundamentally
different document from the traditional job description.

The redesigned job description for a role in a synthetic team has five
components that the traditional job description lacks. The first is the
workflow context: a description of the complete workflow the role
operates within, including the agent teams, the handoffs, and the
governance structure. This context is not background information. It is
the operational reality in which the role functions. A candidate reading
this description should understand that they are being hired to operate
inside a designed system, not to perform a standalone set of tasks.

The second component is the TML layer specification: an explicit
statement of what type of intelligence the role is primarily responsible
for. Is this a role that is primarily in the Task layer, the Management
layer, or the Leadership layer? For most roles in a Co-Intelligent
Company that has run multiple sprints, the answer should be Management
and Leadership: the task-level work has been transferred to agent teams,
and the human role is defined by the judgment-requiring work that
remains. Writing that specification into the job description sends an
organizational signal: we are hiring for judgment, not for task
execution.

The third component is the agent management responsibility: an explicit
statement that the role includes the management and oversight of one or
more agent teams. This is a new responsibility category that did not
exist in traditional job descriptions, and naming it is an act of
organizational acknowledgment. The organization is saying: managing a
synthetic workforce member is a real responsibility, it requires real
skill, and it is something we hire for and evaluate against.

The fourth component is the development trajectory: a description of how
the role is expected to evolve as the sprint program matures. In the
first quarter the role operates at AI-Assisted level. By the third
quarter, as the agent team\'s performance is validated, the role may
transition to monitoring by exception on the task categories. By the
second year, the role may be managing multiple agent teams rather than
one. Writing this trajectory into the job description is an act of
organizational honesty: the role is going to change, and the
organization is telling candidates that change is part of what they are
signing up for.

The fifth component is the feedback loop responsibility: an explicit
statement that the role includes contributing to the sprint
retrospective process and the ongoing improvement of the agent team\'s
design. The human who works alongside an agent team every day is the
organization\'s most valuable source of intelligence about how the agent
is performing and how the design should evolve. Making that intelligence
contribution a named part of the role, not an optional activity, builds
the organizational expectation that human-agent relationship feedback
flows upward through the system.

**12.5.1 What a Redesigned Job Description Looks Like: Elena at
Meridian**

Before the quoting sprint, Elena\'s job description described her role
as VP of Operations at Meridian Manufacturing. Her responsibilities
included: managing the quoting workflow end-to-end, maintaining pricing
accuracy, coordinating with the design engineering team on technical
specifications, managing the shop floor scheduling function, and
developing operational improvement initiatives. The description was
accurate for the work she did before the sprint.

After the quoting sprint, Elena\'s job description for that function
reads differently. Her responsibilities for the quoting workflow now
include: supervising the Quoting Agent team (comprising RFQ Parser,
Material Estimator, and Quote Assembler), reviewing agent-generated
draft quotes for edge cases and relationship-context decisions,
maintaining and updating the pricing rules and exception protocols that
govern the agent team\'s outputs, monitoring the operational log for
quality trends and exception patterns, and contributing to the sprint
retrospective for the quoting workflow. Her workflow context section
describes the full system: the RFQ inputs, the agent team processing
pipeline, her review step, and the quote delivery to Ty for customer
communication.

The TML layer specification for her quoting responsibilities: Management
and Leadership. The agent team handles Task-level execution. Elena
handles Management-level exception processing and Leadership-level
relationship and pricing strategy decisions. The agent management
responsibility section names the three agents she supervises and the
monitoring cadence. The development trajectory section notes that as the
agent team\'s exception rate decreases and Elena\'s confidence in the
output quality increases, the review process will evolve from per-quote
review to sampled review within the next two quarters.

The document is longer than Elena\'s old job description. It is also far
more accurate about what her job actually is and far more useful for
organizational planning, performance management, and hiring decisions.

**12.6 The Development Arc of the Synthetic Team**

Here is a reality that almost no AI implementation framework addresses:
both the human and the agent team will develop over time when they work
together. The agent team develops because the sprint program produces
regular design improvements that extend its capability. The human
develops because the work they are doing becomes progressively more
sophisticated as the task-level work moves to the agent and the
Management and Leadership work expands.

This development is not automatic. It requires organizational attention
and deliberate management. An organization that deploys a sprint and
then manages it as a static system, checking that the workflow is
running and not investing in its development, will see both the agent
team and the human plateau at their initial capability levels. An
organization that actively manages the development of both the agent
team and the human through the sprint retrospective, the one design
change rule, and the quarterly HAC review will see both compound in
capability over time.

The development arc of the synthetic team across six sprints follows a
predictable pattern when the sprint program is managed well. In the
first sprint, the relationship between the human and the agent team is
primarily supervisory at the tactical level: the human reviews every
output, the agent handles the standard cases, and the learning is
concentrated in identifying the edge cases the design did not
anticipate. The human is learning what the agent can and cannot do. The
agent\'s performance is stable but relatively unrefined.

By the second and third sprints, the relationship deepens. The human has
enough experience with the agent\'s outputs to develop a reliable
exception pattern: they know which categories of output to review
carefully and which categories they can approve quickly. The agent team
has been improved through the retrospective process, and its error rate
in the standard cases has decreased. The human begins to move from
tactical supervision toward strategic direction: less time checking
individual outputs, more time thinking about the workflow design and
where the next improvement should be made.

By the fourth and fifth sprints, the relationship matures. The human
manages the agent team primarily by exception, with weekly reviews of
aggregated performance data rather than individual output review. The
agent team is handling a broader range of cases than it did in sprint
one, because the retrospective process has progressively extended the
documented exception protocols. The human\'s work has elevated to the
level the Work Deconstruction specified as the goal of the sprint: more
strategic operations work, more relationship management, more
development of the next sprint\'s design.

By the sixth sprint and beyond, the human-agent team is a stable unit of
organizational capability. The human knows the agent\'s capabilities as
well as they know a long-tenured human team member\'s. They know what it
is excellent at, where it is unreliable, what situations require human
intervention, and what design changes would extend its capability
further. The agent team\'s knowledge layer reflects six quarters of
operational refinement. The collaboration is fluent.

**12.7 Staying Aware of the Synthetic Team\'s Development**

The people leader\'s job is to ensure that both the human and the agent
team are developing as expected and that the organization is staying
aware of the development state of every synthetic team it has deployed.
That awareness requires a standing practice, not a periodic check.

The Hybrid Org Today is the people leader\'s primary tool for synthetic
team awareness. It shows the current state of every active sprint: the
agent teams deployed, their human supervisors, their autonomy levels,
and their last retrospective status. A people leader who reviews the
Hybrid Org Today quarterly has a current picture of every human-agent
team in the organization and can identify teams that are
underperforming, teams that are ready for increased autonomy, and humans
whose role elevation is not materializing as the sprint design intended.

The sprint retrospective is the primary input to the people leader\'s
development intelligence. The Pattern Lift data from every retrospective
tells the people leader whether the humans in the organization are
actually doing the higher work the sprint program was supposed to
create. If the Pattern Lift is not materializing, the people leader
needs to understand why and address it. The most common reasons Pattern
Lift does not materialize are: the higher work was not defined or
assigned (the organization freed the human\'s time but did not give them
anything specific to do with it), the human was not given the
organizational support or authority to perform the higher work (they
have the time but not the positioning), or the higher work does not
exist yet (the constraint that would have needed that work had not yet
been sprinted on).

The one design change rule is the people leader\'s development
investment mechanism for the agent team. Every sprint retrospective
installs one improvement to the agent team\'s design. Over six sprints,
that is six documented improvements to the agent\'s knowledge layer,
decision logic, exception handling, or governance framework. Each
improvement makes the agent more capable and, as a direct consequence,
makes the human\'s role more strategic. The people leader\'s job is to
ensure that the design changes are being made, that they are being made
on the right things (the highest-value improvements, not the most
convenient ones), and that the human supervisor has the organizational
support to implement them.

**12.8 The Synthetic Team Performance Review**

The performance review for a synthetic team is not the performance
review for a human employee. But it should be conducted with the same
organizational seriousness and against the same organizational
standards.

The synthetic team performance review happens quarterly, as part of the
Compound stage retrospective. It evaluates four dimensions. Quality: is
the agent team\'s output meeting the quality standard specified in the
Design Brief? The operational log provides the data. Volume: is the
agent team processing the expected volume of work at the expected
throughput? The workflow metrics provide the data. Error rate: is the
agent team\'s exception and escalation rate within the acceptable range,
and is that range trending in the right direction? The operational log
and the retrospective pattern analysis provide the data. Development:
has the agent team improved in capability since the last review, as
evidenced by a decreased exception rate, an increased volume of cases
handled within standard parameters, or an expanded range of cases the
agent team can handle without escalation? The one design change
implementation provides the data.

The human supervisor\'s performance review includes a new dimension in a
Co-Intelligent Company: synthetic team management effectiveness. How
well is the human supervising their agent team? This is evaluated
against the Right Seat Evaluation dimensions: Are they actively Seeing
It, staying current with the agent\'s outputs and performance trends?
Are they maintaining the Want It accountability, treating the agent\'s
outputs as their organizational responsibility? Are they Suited for It
in practice, making the judgment calls the agent cannot make and
implementing the design improvements the retrospective identifies?

A human supervisor whose agent team is underperforming may be facing a
design problem (the agent was not designed well enough to perform at the
required level), a knowledge problem (the knowledge layer that the agent
depends on has not been maintained), or a management problem (the human
supervisor is not providing effective oversight and improvement).
Distinguishing between these three causes is the people leader\'s
diagnostic responsibility. The response to a design problem is a sprint
redesign. The response to a knowledge problem is a Source refresh. The
response to a management problem is a performance conversation with the
human supervisor.

**12.9 Workforce Planning in a Co-Intelligent Company**

Workforce planning in a Co-Intelligent Company is different from
workforce planning in a human-only organization in one fundamental
respect: the workforce includes synthetic members whose capacity can be
scaled without the hiring process, and whose cost structure is different
from human headcount.

Traditional workforce planning asks: what work needs to be done, and how
many humans will we need to do it? Co-Intelligent workforce planning
asks: what work needs to be done, which of it requires human
intelligence at which TML layer, and what is the optimal combination of
human workers and synthetic agent teams to produce that work at the
required quality and cost?

That second question requires the same capability the sprint program
builds: the ability to analyze work at the task level, to classify tasks
against the TML framework, and to design the human-agent allocation that
produces the required outcome at the lowest appropriate cost. It is the
Signal and Design discipline applied to workforce planning rather than
to individual constraint sprints. The Compounding Scorecard provides the
data: what has each sprint cost, what has each sprint returned, and what
does the trajectory of that data suggest about where future sprint
investments should be directed?

The people leader who understands the sprint program\'s methodology is
the people leader best positioned to contribute to this workforce
planning question. They can read the Hybrid Org Today and identify which
workflows are ready for expanded agent team deployment, which roles are
candidates for Work Deconstruction, and which human capabilities are
becoming more critical as the task-level work moves to the synthetic
workforce. That intelligence is the people leader\'s strategic
contribution to the Co-Intelligent Company\'s growth.

**12.10 Managing the Emotional Dimension of the Synthetic Workforce**

The most organizationally sensitive aspect of the synthetic workforce is
one that no governance framework, no sprint methodology, and no job
description revision can fully address: the human experience of working
alongside AI agents.

The experience is not uniform. Some people in the organization will find
that their work becomes more interesting as the task-level demands
decrease and the judgment-requiring work expands. The Pattern Lift is
real for them, and they will describe the agent team as a genuine
improvement to their professional experience. Some people will find the
transition disorienting: the work they were good at, the tasks that gave
them a sense of competence and contribution, have moved to an agent, and
the higher work is less familiar and less immediately satisfying. Some
people will find the transition threatening: they interpret the agent\'s
presence as evidence that their role is being diminished, that their
expertise is being automated, and that their organizational position is
increasingly precarious.

Each of these experiences is real and each requires a different
organizational response. The people leader\'s job is to stay close
enough to the human-agent relationship in their organization to know
which experience each person is having, and to respond accordingly. The
person for whom the Pattern Lift is working needs recognition and
development support for the higher-level work they are now doing. The
person for whom the transition is disorienting needs explicit support:
clear role definition, active coaching on the new work, and regular
affirmation that the organization values what they bring to it. The
person for whom the transition feels threatening needs a different kind
of conversation: an honest exploration of what the sprint program means
for their role\'s future, what skills will matter going forward, and
what organizational investment exists to help them develop those skills.

The organization that does not have these conversations will experience
the transition as a talent management problem: disengagement, turnover,
and quiet resistance to the sprint program from the people who feel most
threatened by it. The organization that does have these conversations,
actively and with genuine care for the people going through the
transition, will experience the transition as a development opportunity:
a chance to understand each person\'s strengths, to redesign their roles
around those strengths, and to build an organization where every human
is doing the work that genuinely requires them.

That is the people leader\'s role in the Co-Intelligent Company. Not to
manage the AI. Not to manage the technology transition. To manage the
humans who are learning to work alongside the AI, ensuring that they
have the clarity, the support, and the organizational conditions to do
the work that makes them irreplaceable. That is a harder job than
managing a human-only workforce, because it requires the people leader
to stay current with a technology environment that is changing faster
than any previous transition in most organizations\' history. But it is
also a more consequential job, because the organizations that manage
this transition well will have a structural advantage in human capital
that compounds over time, just as the sprint program produces a
structural advantage in operational capability.

**14. Meridian Manufacturing: A Complete Compound Sprint**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Meridian Manufacturing is a fictional company built from real patterns.
Every detail in this case study, the constraint, the team dynamics, the
sprint execution, the artifacts, and the outcomes, reflects the actual
patterns of how Compound Sprints run when they run well. We have used
Meridian throughout every chapter to illustrate individual concepts.
This chapter shows the complete arc, from first session to closed
Compound stage, in the sequence the Sequence actually runs.

Meridian Manufacturing. \$7.2M revenue. Twenty-seven employees. Custom
metal fabrication for industrial clients across the Midwest. Mark
Ellison, CEO, founded the company sixteen years ago and brought in EOS
three years back. Elena Ruiz, VP of Operations, has been with Meridian
for eleven years and is the closest thing the company has to an
institutional memory. Ty Banfield, Sales Lead. Dave Kowalski, Senior
Design Engineer, fourteen years at Meridian, four years from retirement.
Carlos Mendez, Shop Floor Supervisor.

The Sprint Planning Canvas was completed in thirty minutes at the end of
a quarterly offsite. Mark named the constraint from the meeting he had
been in every Tuesday for two years: quoting. Elena looked at the floor
when he said it. Not because she disagreed. Because she knew it was her.

**14.1 Act I: Diagnose**

**14.1.1 Signal**

The Signal session ran for two and a half hours on a Tuesday morning,
six weeks before the sprint was formally committed. The entire
leadership team was present. Mark facilitated the first hour. The second
hour, when the constraint candidate emerged from the Is/Is Not analysis,
was harder.

The candidates that surfaced from the team: quoting speed (Ty,
immediately), Dave\'s retirement risk (Mark, quietly), the floor
scheduling gaps that cost three to four days per quarter in rework
(Carlos, who had been trying to raise this for months). The team ran
Is/Is Not on all three. The quoting Is/Is Not showed that the problem
lived entirely within the quoting workflow and specifically within the
estimation phases: it was NOT present in the sales conversation, NOT
present in the production delivery, NOT present in any other operational
function. The floor scheduling issue showed up differently: it WAS
present in all quarters, NOT isolated to any single product line,
inconsistently severe. The Dave\'s retirement risk did not have an Is/Is
Not pattern: it was everywhere and nowhere, a slow-moving risk rather
than an active constraint.

The Five Whys on the quoting bottleneck: Why does quoting take three to
five business days? Because Elena is the only person who can assemble a
complete quote. Why is Elena the only person? Because the pricing data,
lead-time knowledge, and customer-specific history are all held by her
personally. Why is it held by her personally? Because no system was ever
built to hold it. Why was no system ever built? Because every time
someone started building one, the immediate quoting demand took
priority. Why does the immediate quoting demand always take priority?
Because the customer-facing urgency of any given quote outweighs the
organizational urgency of fixing the underlying structural problem.

The root cause landed cleanly: the quoting function is dependent on one
person\'s undocumented institutional knowledge, creating a structural
bottleneck that cannot be addressed by hiring, by training, or by any
solution that does not first capture and systematize the knowledge that
Elena holds.

The cost: Ty produced the win/loss analysis from the prior eight
quarters. Of the RFQs where Meridian lost to a competitor, forty-one
percent cited quote turnaround time as the primary reason. The average
lost deal value was \$47K. Thirty-seven to forty-two percent of
cancellation slots went unfilled despite a waitlist because the
replacement routing loop ran manually through Elena. The combined cost
estimate: \$558K annually in lost revenue, misallocated time, and floor
underutilization. Mark looked around the room. \'Is this the thing?\'
Nobody hesitated.

The constraint statement: The Meridian quoting function is structurally
bottlenecked by a single point of knowledge dependency (Elena Ruiz, VP
Operations), creating a three-to-five business day turnaround time that
is costing the company an estimated \$558,000 annually in lost bids,
misallocated VP time, and floor scheduling gaps. The bottleneck has
persisted for six or more years and is not addressable through staffing
changes alone because the underlying knowledge is undocumented and
inaccessible without Elena\'s direct involvement.

**14.1.2 Source**

Source ran over nine days across five sessions. Elena was present for
all five. Dave attended one session. Ty attended one session for the CRM
and customer history components.

The Knowledge Map emerged across three sessions with Elena. The first
session mapped the mechanics: Elena\'s step-by-step quoting process, the
systems she touched, the sequence of lookups she performed. The second
session went deeper: the pricing exceptions, the lead-time adjustments
for specific material categories, the customer relationship context that
affected standard pricing. The third session went deeper still: the
judgment calls, the things she did that she could not initially describe
because she had been doing them for so long they were automatic. What
does an RFQ from a new customer look like versus a repeat customer? How
does she decide whether to call Ty before quoting versus quoting and
then following up? When does she involve Dave for a technical
specification question versus relying on the pricing database?

The Knowledge Map: Digital sources (Clean): the CRM (HubSpot, customer
history and contact information), the material pricing spreadsheet
(Elena\'s, seventeen tabs, needs significant curation before agent
access), the job costing system (previous job data, accessible via API).
Digital sources (Needs Work): the material lead-time data (exists as
informal notes in Elena\'s spreadsheet, needs to be structured and
validated). Digital sources (Missing): no formal pricing exception
database, no documented customer-specific terms. Organic sources: Elena
(sole holder of pricing exception logic, customer relationship context,
and job-to-quote mapping judgment), Dave (fabrication complexity
estimation for non-standard specifications). At-risk flags: Elena
(single point of failure across all organic knowledge), Dave (retirement
in four years with no succession plan for technical estimation).

The Source Completeness Test: all digital sources noted with status.
Both organic sources interviewed. Missing sources have capture plans:
the pricing exception database will be built before Design, the
customer-specific terms will be extracted from CRM notes by Ty. At-risk
sources flagged with capture priority. Knowledge Map fits on one page.
Elena can walk a new person through the map without prompting. Source
complete.

**14.2 Act II: Execute and Compound**

**14.2.1 Design**

The Design session ran across four working sessions over ten days. Elena
was present for all four. The technical resource joined for sessions
three and four.

Session one: information flow mapping. The team drew the complete
quoting workflow, from RFQ arrival in email to quote delivery to the
customer. Twenty-two distinct steps. The mapping revealed two things
that Source had hinted at but had not made fully visible. First, twelve
of the twenty-two steps were pure data operations: parsing the RFQ
document, looking up material pricing, calculating standard labor hours,
assembling the draft template. No judgment required. Not even
Management-level judgment. Pure Task execution that Elena was doing
manually because no system had ever been built to do it for her. Second,
the three steps that required genuine judgment were genuinely
irreducible: the pricing decisions for accounts with custom history, the
specification review for non-standard materials, and the
relationship-context call about whether to negotiate or quote standard
for a given account.

Session two: the AI-Assisted versus Automated decision. The conversation
was not technical. It was about trust. Elena was not comfortable with
automated quoting. Not because she did not trust the technology but
because she understood what it meant to send a customer a quote: it was
a commitment made on behalf of Meridian, and she was not prepared to
delegate that commitment to a system she had never seen perform under
real conditions. The compromise was AI-Assisted as the V1 design: the
agent produces every draft, Elena reviews every draft before it leaves
the building. The design includes a transition pathway: after three
months of AI-Assisted operation, if the exception rate is below five
percent and Elena\'s confidence in the agent\'s outputs is high, the
design team will evaluate moving standard-tier accounts to Automated
with Elena reviewing by exception. This was not a compromise forced on
the design. It was the right design for this organization at this
moment.

Session three: the HAC entry and the guardrails. The HAC entry: Function
is Quote Generation. Agent team is the Quoting Agent (three agents: RFQ
Parser, Material Estimator, Quote Assembler). Human Supervisor is Elena
Ruiz. Level is AI-Assisted, transition pathway to Automated documented.
Five guardrails: no quote leaves without Elena\'s review; the agent
cannot access customer payment information or credit terms; any RFQ
flagged as outside standard parameters routes to Elena within the same
business day; Elena reviews the operational log every Monday morning;
the kill switch triggers if material estimate error rate exceeds five
percent in any given week.

Session four: Design Brief completion and Design Gate review. All five
Design Gate items passed. The Design Brief was distributed to the
technical resource before the session closed. Build began the following
Monday.

**14.2.2 Build**

Build ran for three weeks. The technical resource was a freelance n8n
developer who had worked with Jesse\'s company on a prior project and
understood the Compound Sprint\'s Build philosophy: execute the Design
Brief, don\'t make design decisions.

Week one: knowledge layer construction. The material pricing spreadsheet
was extracted from Excel, cleaned, and imported into Airtable with a
structured schema that the agent could query programmatically. The
pricing exception database was built from the sessions with Elena:
twenty-three exception categories documented, validated by Elena against
her memory of the prior two years\' quoting history, structured for
agent access. The customer-specific terms were extracted from CRM notes
by Ty, reviewed by Elena, and added to the knowledge layer. The CRM
connection was established. The job costing system API was connected. No
agent was deployed in week one. The knowledge layer was built first.

Week two: agent workflow construction. Three agents were built in n8n.
The RFQ Parser took incoming RFQ documents, extracted the relevant
specifications, and structured them for the downstream agents. The
Material Estimator looked up pricing and lead times from the knowledge
layer, applied the documented exception rules, and flagged any inputs
outside the standard parameters. The Quote Assembler took the parser\'s
structural output and the estimator\'s pricing output, combined them
with customer history from the CRM, and assembled the draft quote in
Meridian\'s standard format.

Integration testing at the end of week two ran the full workflow against
ten RFQs from the prior quarter, including two edge cases that Elena had
flagged during the exception protocol documentation. One edge case
handled correctly. One edge case produced an incorrect material estimate
because a pricing rule had a condition that the documentation had
captured incorrectly. The technical resource surfaced the gap to Elena.
Elena corrected the documentation. The agent was updated. The second
integration test passed.

Week three: Human Orchestrator handoff and operational log setup. Elena
ran the complete workflow independently against ten new RFQs under the
technical resource\'s observation. She identified two additional edge
cases that the test environment had not surfaced, both handled correctly
by the escalation protocol. She requested one change to the draft quote
format: a section header that the agent was producing in the wrong
typeface for Meridian\'s standard template. The change took thirty
minutes. The operational log was configured and its four categories
confirmed. Elena reviewed the log for the first time against the week\'s
test outputs. Build done on day nineteen.

**14.2.3 Deliver**

Deliver began on a Wednesday. Mark sent a company-wide note the evening
before: the quoting workflow is changing. Starting tomorrow, quotes are
generated by a new agent team and reviewed by Elena before delivery. The
process is the same for everyone interacting with quotes: RFQs still go
to the same email address, quotes still come from Elena. What is
different is what happens in between.

Three per-role runbooks were distributed. Elena\'s covered her
four-hours-per-week review process in detail. Ty\'s covered the new RFQ
routing protocol and the customer communication approach during the
one-to-two business day exception handling window. Dave\'s covered the
new structured input format for technical specifications and the
escalation path for non-standard materials.

The first live quote took fourteen minutes. Elena said: \'That took me
fourteen minutes. It would have taken me three hours.\' She approved the
quote and it went to the customer. The customer responded within the
hour: can Meridian have the production slot available in six weeks?

The first two weeks of operation: six edge cases logged. All six routed
correctly through the escalation protocol. One unplanned intervention: a
repeat customer called Elena directly for a quote status update, out of
habit, before Ty had updated the customer with the standard protocol.
Elena noted it in the log under user confusion. She called Ty afterward
and they adjusted the communication protocol for repeat customers.

Delivery Test at the end of week two: all items passed. The sprint is
delivered.

**14.2.4 Compound**

The Sprint Retrospective ran two weeks after the Delivery Test passed.
Elena, Mark, Ty, and Dave were present. The retrospective took ninety
minutes.

Pattern Drag: three instances. Two team members had sent RFQs directly
to Elena instead of routing through the standard queue during the first
week. Elena had answered both out of reflex. By week two, the queue
routing was holding. The third instance was Ty: he was using the old RFQ
email format, which required Elena to manually extract the
specifications before the RFQ Parser could process it. The documentation
had described the new format but the training had not been specific
enough about the consequences of the old format. An update to Ty\'s
runbook addressed this before the retrospective closed.

Pattern Gap: one. The multi-site RFQ format, for customers ordering
materials across two or more production locations, had not been
accounted for in the Design. Multi-site RFQs represented eleven percent
of the total RFQ volume and were all routing to manual handling. The
design could accommodate them with an additional parsing rule, but the
rule had not been specified because the multi-site format had not
appeared in the ten-RFQ test set. This was flagged as the one design
change.

Pattern Lift: confirmed. Elena\'s Monday meeting with Mark, the one she
had been scheduling for two years to discuss strategic operations
priorities but repeatedly canceling because quoting consumed the time,
happened for the first time three weeks after the sprint went live. She
came with a vendor renegotiation analysis she had been building in the
eleven hours per week the sprint had freed. Mark extended the meeting.
\'This is the conversation I have been trying to have with you for two
years.\'

Constraint Re-rank: the quoting bottleneck was closed. The new top
constraint from the updated backlog: Dave\'s retirement risk and the
technical estimation knowledge that would leave with him. Sprint two was
committed before the retrospective closed. Start date: three weeks from
today. Human Orchestrator: Dave, with Elena as leadership sponsor.

Compounding Scorecard at sprint one: Signal estimate \$558K, Deliver
result \$412K recovered in year one, delta \$412K. Sprint two scheduled.

**15. Case Study: The PM Agent Team**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The second case study is a different kind of company from Meridian: a
software services firm rather than a manufacturing operation, a project
coordination constraint rather than a quoting bottleneck, and a Human
Orchestrator who built the sprint workflow herself rather than
supervising a freelance developer. The sprint produced a different kind
of result from Meridian\'s, but it followed the same Sequence and
demonstrated the same compounding principle: each sprint leaves behind
infrastructure the next one builds on.

The company: a twelve-person software services firm specializing in
website and digital platform development for professional services
clients. Revenue approximately \$1.8M annually. The founding team of
four is complemented by a mix of contract developers, a content lead,
and Sofia Reyes, VP of Operations, who manages every client engagement
from contract through delivery.

The constraint: the project coordination function. Task updates not
happening on time. Client deliverable tracking inconsistent. Billing
delayed because nobody could confirm what had been delivered and what
had not. A project coordination subcontractor was in place, costing
\$24,000 per year, and the work was still falling behind.

**15.1 Signal: Naming the Constraint**

The Signal process began with a question Sofia had never asked formally
but had been living with for two years: what is this actually costing?

The subcontractor\'s invoice was \$24,000. That was the visible cost.
The Five Whys revealed the invisible costs. Sofia was spending six hours
per week chasing task status because the subcontractor\'s updates were
incomplete. Six hours per week of VP of Operations time at Sofia\'s
billing rate represented approximately \$39,000 annually. Late billing,
caused by the inability to confirm delivery status in time for the
monthly invoice cycle, was producing payment delays of fifteen to thirty
days on an average of forty percent of invoices. The average invoice
value was \$12,000. A thirty-day delay on forty percent of invoices
represented approximately \$24,000 in annual cash flow cost.

Total constraint cost: approximately \$87,000 annually, against a
\$24,000 subcontractor budget and a widely shared organizational sense
that the problem was primarily a personnel issue rather than a
structural one.

The constraint statement: The project coordination function at the
company produces inconsistent task updates, delayed billing, and VP of
Operations escalation time estimated at six hours per week, totaling
approximately \$87,000 in annual costs. The constraint is structural:
the role requires constant, systematic attention to task status that
cannot be sustained by a part-time resource at the compensation level
the function justifies as a standalone role.

**15.2 Source: Mapping the Knowledge**

Source was the most revealing stage of this sprint, for a reason that
the Signal analysis had hinted at but not fully surfaced. The knowledge
in the project coordination role was not complex. It was not tribal. It
was not concentrated in any single person\'s judgment or experience. It
was procedural.

The process of mapping what the project coordinator actually did
produced eighteen distinct tasks. Fourteen of the eighteen were pure
data operations: reading task status from the project management tool,
updating records in the CRM, generating status reports from a template,
routing exception notifications to the correct person. No judgment
required. Not even the Management-level judgment that the TML framework
identifies as the AI-Assisted zone. Pure Task execution.

The remaining four tasks did require judgment: deciding whether a
project that was behind schedule needed to be escalated to the CEO or
could be managed within Sofia\'s authority, communicating with clients
when a deliverable was late in a way that preserved the relationship
while managing expectations, reviewing invoices before they went out for
accuracy and completeness, and making the weekly decision about which
projects needed proactive attention.

The Source insight: the organization had been paying a human being to do
fourteen tasks that required no human intelligence and four tasks that
required human judgment. The fourteen task-level items were consuming
seventy percent of the subcontractor\'s time. The four judgment items
were receiving thirty percent of the time, which was not enough. The
structural problem was not the subcontractor\'s performance. It was the
role design.

Missing sources: none significant. The project management tool had
complete task data. The CRM had complete client and billing data. The
communication templates were documented. The escalation protocol was
informal but reproducible. For once, a Source run found that the
knowledge was fully accessible because the work it supported was
entirely procedural.

**15.3 Design: Allocating the Work**

Work Deconstruction took ninety minutes. Sofia had done enough
preparation before the session that the classification decisions were
mostly confirmations rather than debates.

Two tasks were classified as Human Judgment Required: the escalation
decision and the client relationship communication when a deliverable
was late. Both required Sofia\'s authority, her relationship context,
and her judgment about what the client relationship could absorb. Both
stayed entirely with Sofia.

Two tasks were classified as AI-Assisted: the weekly status report that
went to the CEO (agent drafts based on project management data, Sofia
reviews before sending) and the invoice review (agent generates the
invoice from billing data, Sofia reviews before sending). Both were
judgment-requiring at the Management level: the agent could produce a
technically correct output but Sofia needed to validate it before it had
external consequence.

The remaining fourteen tasks were split between Fully Automated (ten
tasks) and Workflow Automation Only (four tasks). The ten automated
tasks included all of the task status update processing, the data entry
across systems, the exception flagging and routing, and the generation
of internal project status reports. The four workflow automation only
tasks were pure data routing: moving task status changes from the
project management tool to the CRM, triggering billing reminders at
defined intervals, and routing client communication from the project
management tool to the correct team member.

The HAC entry: Function is Project Coordination. Agent team is the PM
Agent Team (two agents: Status Manager and Report Generator, plus one
workflow automation layer). Human Supervisor is Sofia Reyes. Level is
Automated for ten task categories, AI-Assisted for two, Human Judgment
Required for two. Sofia monitors the Automated category by exception via
the operational log. She reviews the AI-Assisted outputs before they
have external consequence.

**15.4 Build: What Got Built**

Sofia built this sprint herself. Not with developer support. With n8n, a
Claude integration, and three weeks of learning-by-doing that she
described afterward as the hardest and most valuable thing she had done
in five years of running the company\'s operations.

Phase one failed. The initial approach connected n8n to the project
management tool, extracted task status data, and used Claude to generate
a formatted status report. It worked in the test environment. In
production, it produced incorrect reports on the first day because the
task data in the project management tool was inconsistently formatted by
different developers who had their own conventions for naming completed
tasks. The problem was not the agent. The problem was the data quality.
Sofia shut down phase one after day two.

The data quality gap was a Source failure: the team had assumed the task
data was clean and consistent without validating it. Sofia convened a
thirty-minute team meeting and introduced a unified task naming
protocol: all completed tasks use a specific status tag, all in-progress
tasks use a different tag, all blocked tasks use a third. One week of
manual enforcement later, the data was consistent enough to build
against.

Phase two worked. The Status Manager agent ran every morning at seven,
pulling task data from the project management tool, identifying tasks
that were behind schedule or overdue, classifying them against the
escalation protocol, and routing exception notifications to the correct
person. The Report Generator ran on Thursdays at five, pulling the
week\'s project data, generating the client status reports from the
template, and placing them in Sofia\'s review queue for Friday morning.
The workflow automation layer handled the CRM updates, the billing
reminders, and the internal communication routing.

The subcontractor contract was not renewed at its monthly renewal point,
six weeks after phase two went live.

**15.5 Deliver: The First Six Weeks**

The per-role runbooks for Deliver were written entirely by Sofia. One
for herself (her review cadence, her escalation decision criteria, her
invoice review process). One for the development team (the new task
naming protocol and its consequences for the agent\'s accuracy). One for
the CEO (the new format of the weekly status report and the fact that it
was agent-generated and Sofia-reviewed).

Week one adoption rate: ninety-four percent of tasks updated using the
documented format. The six percent non-compliance was three developers
who had not attended the protocol meeting and were using the old format.
Sofia sent a brief message. Week two adoption rate: one hundred percent.

The operational log captured twelve events in the first six weeks: seven
edge cases (all handled correctly by the escalation protocol), three
user confusion events (all resolved within twenty-four hours with a
runbook clarification), and two unplanned interventions (one escalation
that Sofia handled directly when the CEO was copied on a client
communication before Sofia had reviewed it, and one billing delay when
the invoice generation ran on a day when the project management data had
a sync gap). Both unplanned interventions were design gaps that the
retrospective addressed.

The measurement at six weeks: Sofia\'s project coordination overhead had
dropped from six hours per week to fifty minutes per week. Billing
delays of fifteen-plus days dropped from forty percent of invoices to
eight percent. The subcontractor cost was eliminated. Net annual
savings: approximately \$68,000 against the \$87,000 Signal estimate,
with the remaining gap attributable to the two design gaps that the
Compound stage would address.

**15.6 Compound: What the Next Sprint Inherits**

The Sprint Retrospective for the PM Agent Team sprint ran three weeks
after the six-week measurement period completed. Sofia, the CEO, and one
senior developer were present.

Pattern Drag: two instances, both resolved before the retrospective. The
task naming protocol non-compliance in week one had been addressed
immediately. The CEO had been sending a few project status questions
directly to the developers instead of checking the agent-generated
weekly report, but stopped after week three when the reports had proven
reliable enough to trust.

Pattern Gap: two design gaps. The unplanned intervention when the CEO
was copied before Sofia\'s review indicated that the review queue
notification was not prominent enough: Sofia sometimes cleared her
review queue at the end of the day rather than at the start, and on one
occasion the workflow had processed a report before she had reviewed it.
The fix was a notification change, not a workflow change. The sync gap
that caused the billing delay indicated that the workflow needed a data
freshness check before generating invoices: if the sync had not
completed within the prior four hours, the invoice generation should
pause and notify Sofia.

Pattern Lift: Sofia\'s six recovered hours per week had gone into client
relationship management that she had been deferring for months. Three
client relationships that had been on her watch list for signs of churn
received proactive attention in the first six weeks. None churned. One
renewed for an expanded engagement. The pattern lift was directly
attributable to the sprint.

The one design change: the notification timing change to ensure Sofia\'s
review happened before reports were distributed. Implemented within two
days of the retrospective. Verified before sprint two was committed.

Constraint Re-rank: the project coordination constraint was closed. The
new top constraint: the client onboarding workflow, which had been on
the Signal Backlog since sprint one\'s original re-rank. Sprint two
committed. Human Orchestrator: Sofia again. Start date: four weeks. The
Knowledge Map that Source would have taken three weeks to build from
scratch in sprint one was already sixty percent complete from the PM
Agent Team sprint. Sprint two\'s Source session took forty-five minutes.

**16. Key Terminology**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This glossary defines the vocabulary used throughout the book: both
terms coined for this framework and technical terms borrowed from AI and
software practice. Terms marked (Compound) are specific to the framework
described in this book. Terms marked (Technical) are industry-standard
vocabulary explained for non-technical readers.

**16.1 Compound Framework Terms**

**AI Readiness Scorecard**

> A five-dimension diagnostic tool covering Constraint Clarity,
> Information Readiness, Workflow Visibility, Decision Rights, and
> Measurement Discipline, scored Red/Yellow/Green to show a leadership
> team exactly where their AI readiness gaps are before starting a
> sprint.

**At-Risk Source**

> Institutional knowledge that lives in one person\'s head and is about
> to leave the organization through departure, retirement, or role
> change. Named and prioritized during Source so it can be captured
> before it walks out the door.

**Clarity Call**

> A 30-minute, no-cost conversation with a Compound strategist designed
> to pressure-test a constraint and determine whether it is
> sprint-ready. Not a sales pitch; the output is a named constraint you
> can act on.

**Co-Intelligence**

> The designed structure where human and agent intelligence work
> together against the same problems inside the same operating loop. The
> system half of the equation Co-Intelligence + Rhythm = Compound.

**Co-Intelligent Co-operation**

> The activity of humans and AI working the same problems together,
> inside the same loop, against the same outcome, as the company\'s
> default way of working rather than a project or initiative.

**Co-Intelligent Company**

> What a company becomes when humans and AI work as one system, not two
> parallel tracks. The bar is higher than \'uses AI tools.\' It means
> the accountability chart has been explicitly extended to include agent
> teams alongside humans.

**Co-Operating Model**

> The explicit design of who brings what intelligence to the work and
> who owns which operating responsibility. The structure that makes
> Co-Intelligent Co-operation possible and the answer to the
> operating-model problem most companies have.

**Compound (stage)**

> The sixth and final stage of the Sequence. After a sprint is
> delivered, Compound runs the Sprint Retrospective, re-ranks the Signal
> Backlog, installs one design change, updates the Hybrid Accountability
> Chart, and names the next constraint.

**Compound Bench**

> Six AI coaching agents, one per stage of the Sequence, that run the
> framework with you during a sprint: interrogating constraints,
> surfacing knowledge gaps, running Work Deconstruction, specifying
> builds, structuring rollouts, and closing the retrospective.

**Compound Sprint**

> One complete pass through the six-stage Sequence (Signal, Source,
> Design, Build, Deliver, Compound) on one validated constraint. The
> unit of work this book teaches.

**Compounding Scorecard**

> A running table that tracks the cost delta for each sprint across
> quarters: constraint solved, cost before, cost after, delta, and
> cumulative impact, so leadership can see the compounding returns
> building over time.

**Constraint Statement**

> The one-page artifact that Signal produces: a single sentence naming
> the operational constraint, plus the location in the org, its
> duration, the quantified cost, and the validating evidence. The
> handoff from Signal to Source.

**COE Operating Model**

> Centers of Excellence operating model: the organizational design
> structure that coordinates specialized functions across a large
> organization with defined governance, accountability, and quarterly
> alignment sessions. The governance template for the Compound quarterly
> operating session.

**Decision Rights**

> One of the five dimensions of the AI Readiness Scorecard. The degree
> to which a leadership team has explicitly assigned which tasks AI
> handles versus which stay with humans, and who has authority to change
> the boundary.

**Design Brief**

> The comprehensive artifact produced in Design that captures all design
> decisions before Build: workflow summary, stakeholders, systems
> involved, data requirements, success criteria, constraints and
> guardrails, and V1 artifact description.

**Design Gate**

> The five-item checklist that must be fully checked before a sprint
> moves from Design into Build: Work Deconstruction complete, all HAC
> fields filled with named supervisors, Human Orchestrator named,
> AI-Assisted versus Automated decided with rationale, and guardrails
> defined.

**Human Orchestrator**

> The single person who owns both irreducible human decisions and agent
> team supervision for a sprint. Sets goals, reviews at the goal level,
> manages the agent team day-to-day, surfaces escalations, and makes
> design improvements between sprints.

**Hybrid Accountability Chart (HAC)**

> An extension of a traditional accountability chart that includes agent
> teams alongside humans. Every row names a function or agent team, the
> human supervisor, and the AI-Assisted versus Automated level. Every
> row has a named supervisor. No exceptions.

**Hybrid Org Today**

> A one-page living document that gives the current operational picture
> of the Co-Intelligent Company: active sprint status, Signal Backlog
> ranked by cost, Hybrid Accountability Chart entries, completed sprint
> outcomes, and the next quarterly review date.

**Knowledge Map**

> The one-page Source deliverable showing what the organization knows
> about the validated constraint: every relevant data source, its owner,
> its status (Clean/Needs Work/Missing), and its pipeline connectivity.

**Organizational Memory**

> The layer of connected knowledge, CRM records, SOPs, transcripts,
> client history, that makes an agent know a company rather than just
> the world. Built deliberately through Source and maintained sprint by
> sprint.

**Parallel Workstreams**

> Multiple agent-driven work tracks running simultaneously under one
> human\'s direction. The design pattern that collapses calendar time
> and produces compounding output, contrasted with linear work where
> tasks run sequentially.

**Pattern Drag**

> Old habits and informal structures that resist new workflow designs
> after they are deployed. The most common cause of Deliver failure.
> Must be identified in the Design Gate check and countered by the
> governance structure before Build completes.

**Pattern Gap**

> The difference between the workflow as it was designed and the
> workflow as it is actually being run in production. Pattern Gap
> analysis in the retrospective identifies whether the gap is a design
> error, a training error, or a governance error.

**Pattern Lift**

> The observable elevation of human work quality and scope when agents
> take the routine tasks the human was previously performing. The the
> accountability structure return on the sprint investment: not just
> that a workflow improved, but that the humans are doing work that
> requires more of what makes them irreplaceable.

**Rhythm**

> The quarterly cadence of running sprints. What turns a single sprint
> into a compounding business outcome. Running the Sequence over and
> over, with a quarterly operating session to review the previous
> sprint, update the Signal Backlog, commit the next sprint, and update
> the HAC.

**Right Seat Evaluation**

> Three-test framework for assigning human accountability in the Hybrid
> Accountability Chart: Sees It (can the supervisor evaluate agent
> output quality?), Wants It (is this person genuinely accountable, not
> just named?), and Suited for It (do they have the judgment and
> authority to make calls the agent cannot make?).

**Sequence**

> The ordered six stages inside the Framework: Signal, Source, Design,
> Build, Deliver, Compound. Always all six. Always in that order,
> because each stage produces something the next stage requires.

**Signal (stage)**

> Stage 1 of the Sequence. The discipline of identifying the one
> operational constraint worth solving, tracing symptoms to root cause,
> and quantifying the cost in dollars or hours before committing a
> sprint to it. Produces a one-page Constraint Statement.

**Signal Backlog**

> The running, ranked list of validated operational constraints the
> organization is working through. Updated during every Compound
> session. The top entry becomes the input for the next sprint\'s Signal
> conversation.

**Source (stage)**

> Stage 2 of the Sequence. Maps the information environment around the
> validated constraint: what knowledge exists, where it lives, who holds
> it, what is missing, and what is at risk of leaving. Produces the
> Knowledge Map.

**Sprint Planning Canvas**

> A one-page artifact completed before Signal begins: names the
> constraint, maps a first-pass answer to each stage, identifies the
> team, sets the Human Orchestrator, and books the quarterly review.

**Sprint Retrospective**

> The structured review that runs inside the Compound stage using three
> lenses: Pattern Drag (where did old habits resist the new design?),
> Pattern Gap (where does the actual workflow differ from the designed
> workflow?), and Pattern Lift (what higher-leverage work are humans now
> doing with the capacity the agent returned?).

**TML Framework**

> Task/Management/Leadership work design model: a three-layer
> classification system separating organizational work by the type of
> intelligence required. Task work follows defined rules and can be
> fully systematized. Management work requires judgment about process
> and is best handled with human-AI collaboration. Leadership work
> requires judgment about people, strategy, and context and must remain
> with humans.

**PIS Framework**

> Problem/Identify/Solution diagnostic structure: a three-phase approach
> that prevents organizations from moving to solution before the problem
> is fully understood. Signal is the Problem phase, Source is the
> Identify phase, and Design through Deliver is the Solution phase.

**Work Deconstruction**

> The Design-stage instrument that classifies every task in a constraint
> workflow into four categories: Human judgment required,
> Agent-assisted, Fully automatable, or Workflow automation only, to
> determine what the agent team handles and what stays with a human.

**16.2 Technical Terms**

**API (Application Programming Interface)**

> How software systems talk to each other. Think of a waiter carrying
> orders between a kitchen and a table: the API carries requests from
> one system and brings structured answers back from another.

**Chunking**

> How documents are broken into smaller pieces for storage and retrieval
> in a RAG system. The size and structure of chunks affects how
> accurately the AI finds and uses information.

**Context Engineering**

> The practice of designing what information an AI model has access to
> and how it is structured: context windows, system prompts, project
> instructions, knowledge bases, and RAG configurations. Distinct from
> prompt engineering.

**Context Window**

> The maximum amount of text an AI model can see at one time. Longer
> context windows let the model work with larger documents or longer
> conversations.

**CRM**

> Customer Relationship Management software that stores and manages a
> company\'s relationships with customers and prospects. Examples:
> HubSpot, Salesforce.

**Data Pipeline**

> An automated flow that moves data from one system to another, often
> transforming or processing it along the way.

**Embeddings**

> A way of representing words, sentences, or documents as lists of
> numbers so a computer can measure how similar two pieces of text are.
> Used in vector databases and RAG systems.

**Fine-tuning**

> Retraining an AI model on your company\'s own data so it behaves
> differently by default. Expensive, slow to set up, and hard to update.
> Contrast with RAG, which is faster and more flexible for most business
> use cases.

**Hallucination**

> When an AI model produces a confident, plausible-sounding answer that
> is factually wrong. A core reason human review of agent output
> matters.

**Large Language Model (LLM)**

> The AI technology underneath tools like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini.
> Trained on enormous amounts of text to recognize and generate language
> at scale.

**Low-code / No-code**

> Software development approaches that let non-developers build
> functional workflows using visual interfaces and pre-built components.
> Examples: Zapier, Make, Airtable, n8n.

**MCP (Model Context Protocol)**

> How an AI model connects to your company\'s tools and live data,
> reaching into your CRM, reading your knowledge base, or checking a
> project management tool in real time during a workflow.

**RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)**

> Giving an AI model access to your company\'s specific documents and
> data at the moment it needs them, without retraining the model. Faster
> and cheaper to set up than fine-tuning, and easy to update.

**SOP (Standard Operating Procedure)**

> A documented, step-by-step description of how a process is performed.
> A well-written SOP is the foundation for an AI agent that executes
> that process consistently.

**Token**

> The basic unit AI models use to process text, roughly three-quarters
> of a word. Every prompt you send and every response generated consumes
> tokens, and providers charge per token.

**Vector Database**

> A type of database that stores information as embeddings, allowing
> fast similarity searches. Used in RAG systems so agents can quickly
> retrieve relevant knowledge from large document collections.

**Webhook**

> A mechanism that lets one software system automatically notify another
> when something happens. Webhooks are the \'if this, then that\'
> triggers that power many workflow automations.

**17. Action Step Index**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This appendix collects every action step from the book in sequence. Use
it as a checklist: work through the steps that match wherever you are in
the Sequence. If you are running your first sprint, start with the
Signal steps and work forward. If you are mid-Rhythm, use this as a
review to catch anything you skipped.

**17.1 Chapter 1: Diagnosis**

> • Score your organization against all five AI Readiness Scorecard
> dimensions.
>
> • Do this with your full leadership team, not alone.
>
> • Document the scores and identify your highest Yellow (your starting
> point).
>
> • Fill in the Sprint Planning Canvas with your top constraint
> candidate.

**17.2 Chapter 2: The Co-Operating Model**

> • Pick one role in your organization and sort its weekly tasks into
> Human Intelligence and Agent Intelligence columns.
>
> • Calculate the percentage of that role\'s week that falls in the
> Agent column.
>
> • Identify the one function in your company where parallel workstreams
> would produce the highest return.
>
> • Map the current handoffs in that function and note where
> accountability is undefined.

**17.3 Chapter 3: The Framework**

> • Fill in the Sprint Planning Canvas for your top constraint
> candidate.
>
> • Name the leadership sponsor and the Human Orchestrator.
>
> • Book the quarterly review on the calendar before Signal begins.
>
> • Run the Pre-Flight Checklist. Resolve every unchecked box before
> proceeding.

**17.4 Chapter 4: Signal**

> • Facilitate the constraint-surfacing session with your leadership
> team.
>
> • Run the Is/Is Not test and the Five Whys on your top three
> candidates.
>
> • Run the five constraint questions on the surviving candidates.
>
> • Pick one. Write the constraint statement in one sentence.
>
> • Put a cost on it. Even a rough estimate.
>
> • Get leadership team agreement. Lock the constraint.
>
> • Run the failure mode checklist. Fix every failure mode before moving
> to Source.

**17.5 Chapter 5: Source**

> • Build the Knowledge Map for the validated constraint.
>
> • Interview every organic source. Transcribe the conversations.
>
> • Classify every source as Digital, Organic, or Missing.
>
> • Flag every At-Risk Source.
>
> • Run the Source Completeness Test. Fix every failure before moving to
> Design.

**17.6 Chapter 6: Designing the System**

> • Produce the Hybrid Accountability Chart entry for the sprint.
>
> • Apply the Right Seat Evaluation to the named Human Orchestrator.
>
> • Make the AI-Assisted versus Automated decision for each agent team.
>
> • Define the governance and guardrails, including the kill switch.
>
> • Produce the information flow specification for the constraint
> workflow.

**17.7 Chapter 7: Designing the Work**

> • Run Work Deconstruction on every task in the constraint workflow.
>
> • Build the swim lane diagram showing human and agent lanes.
>
> • Produce the Design Brief.
>
> • Prototype the agent interaction in a conversational interface before
> Build begins.
>
> • Run the Design Gate checklist. Do not proceed to Build until all
> five items are complete.

**17.8 Chapter 8: Build**

> • Select the build path appropriate to the design.
>
> • Produce the Build Spec covering all eight sections.
>
> • Run the Guardrails Checklist.
>
> • Run the Implementation Mistakes Checklist.
>
> • Run the done test (five questions) against last week\'s actual data.
>
> • Confirm the Human Orchestrator can run the workflow without the
> builder present.

**17.9 Chapter 9: Deliver**

> • Run the Deploy Readiness Audit.
>
> • Produce per-role runbooks for every role whose work changes.
>
> • Train every affected role on the new handoff, not just the new tool.
>
> • Set up the operational log before go-live.
>
> • Document the measurement baseline.
>
> • Run the Delivery Test at the end of the first week live.
>
> • Measure the Pattern Lift at the end of week two.

**17.10 Chapter 10: Compound**

> • Run the Sprint Retrospective using Pattern Drag, Pattern Gap, and
> Pattern Lift lenses.
>
> • Identify one design change to install before the next sprint begins.
>
> • Install the design change. Verify it is in place.
>
> • Re-rank the Signal Backlog.
>
> • Update the Hybrid Accountability Chart.
>
> • Update the Knowledge Map.
>
> • Update the Hybrid Org Today.
>
> • Update the Compounding Scorecard.

**17.11 Chapter 11: The Rhythm**

> • Schedule the quarterly operating session before the current sprint
> ends.
>
> • Bring the Compounding Scorecard, the updated Hybrid Org Today, and
> the re-ranked Signal Backlog to the session.
>
> • Commit the next sprint: named constraint, named Human Orchestrator,
> named start date, named review date.
>
> • Leave the session with the Sprint Planning Canvas for sprint two
> complete.

**17.12 Chapter 12: What to Do Next**

> • Write down your top constraint in one sentence.
>
> • Put a number on what it costs per year.
>
> • Name the Human Orchestrator.
>
> • Name the leadership sponsor.
>
> • Name the start date.
>
> • Name the quarterly review date.
>
> • Book the Clarity Call at compoundorg.com/clarity-call if you want to
> run the first sprint with direct support.
>
> • Start compounding.
