**1.3 The Wrong Question**

There is one specific trap every organization falls into at the start of
its AI effort: it looks exactly like the right approach.

The first question most teams ask is task-oriented: which of our
existing tasks could AI help with? It sounds reasonable. It sounds
practical. And it is not wrong --- it gets you some of the way there.
You will find prompt tips, a few useful shortcuts, some real time saved.
But task-orientation is woefully insufficient. It adds AI on top of the
existing workflow. The existing workflow was designed for humans. So the
gains stay small, the wins stay scattered, and nothing changes
structurally.

The question that unlocks real value is goal-oriented: what outcomes are
we accountable for, and how should the human and AI workforce be
designed to deliver them?

**8.1 Training the People Whose Handoffs Change**

focuses on how to use the new system. The training that prevents
adoption failure focuses on where the human\'s work ends and the

highest failure risk in any human-AI workflow. It is where the human
either completes the output correctly and passes it to the agent, or
does it incorrectly and compromises everything the agent produces
downstream. Training that teaches the tool but does not teach the
handoff produces people who know how to access the system and do not
know how to work with it correctly. Every person whose handoff changes
in Deliver should be able to answer two questions before the workflow
goes live: what do I do differently now, and what does the agent do that

incomplete.]{.underline}]{.insertion author="Julie Mann"

**9. Compound**

[[Julie:]{.underline}]{.insertion author="Julie Mann"
date="2026-05-23T00:00:00Z"}

[[The Compound stage is where organizational learning happens --- or
does not. In every operating model transformation I have led, the
difference between organizations that sustained the change and
organizations that reverted was not the quality of the initial
implementation. It was whether the organization built a mechanism to
learn from the implementation and carry that learning into the next
cycle. EOS calls this the same system in the quarterly operating

**9.1 The Sprint Retrospective --- Three Lenses**

[[Julie\'s Pattern Method provides three retrospective lenses that
structure the Compound sprint review. Pattern Drag: where did old habits
resist the new design? Which team members routed around the agent
workflow? Which supervisors approved output without reviewing it? Which
process owners reverted to manual work at the first friction point? Name
every instance. Each one is a design improvement opportunity. Pattern
Gap: where is the workflow as it is actually being run different from
the workflow as it was designed? Every gap between design and reality is
information. Some gaps mean the design was wrong. Some gaps mean the
training was wrong. Some gaps mean the guardrails were in the wrong

higher-leverage work are the humans now doing with the time and capacity
the agent returned to them? If the lift is happening, name it. It is the
sprint\'s organizational return and it belongs in the next sprint\'s

date="2026-05-23T00:00:00Z"}

**9.2 The One Design Change**

Every retrospective installs exactly one design change before the next
sprint begins.

[[The discipline of one design change per retrospective comes from

improvements were made. The list became the backlog. The backlog
accumulated. The next sprint began with the same design failures as the
last one. One change, made and confirmed before the next sprint begins,
is the discipline that produces compounding improvement. The change does
not have to be large. It has to be real --- implemented, verified, and
incorporated into the updated HAC and Knowledge Map before the next
sprint\'s Signal conversation begins. The Compound stage is not complete
until the one design change is installed. An insight without an
installation is not a retrospective. It is a

**10. The Rhythm**

The Rhythm is what turns a single sprint into a compounding business.
Quarterly. On a cadence. Against real constraints.

[[Julie:]{.underline}]{.insertion author="Julie Mann"
date="2026-05-23T00:00:00Z"}

[[The quarterly cadence is not arbitrary. It is the tempo at which
organizational change can be absorbed, validated, and extended without
overwhelming the team\'s capacity to change. In every operating model
transformation I have led, the organizations that tried to change faster
than quarterly produced change that did not hold. The team could not
absorb the new design before the next change arrived. The organizations
that tried to change slower than quarterly lost momentum. The sprint
learning did not compound because too much time passed between cycles
for the previous sprint\'s improvements to stay fresh. Quarterly is the
tempo because it matches the natural rhythm of organizational
decision-making: four times per year, leadership teams step back from
execution to evaluate what is working and set the next priority. The
Compound Sprint installs its compounding logic into that existing rhythm
rather than creating a new one. The result is a program that runs
alongside the operating cadence the organization already has, not in

**10.2 The Compounding Scorecard**

**12. New Case Study: A Professional Services Sprint --- The Succession

[[Julie:]{.underline}]{.insertion author="Julie Mann"
date="2026-05-23T00:00:00Z"}

[[This case study is drawn from a pattern I have encountered in
professional services and advisory firms across multiple engagements.
The constraint is always a variation of the same structural failure: a
firm where the senior practitioners hold the entire client relationship
in their heads, where every piece of institutional knowledge about the
client\'s situation, their preferences, their history, and the work
completed on their behalf lives in one person and has no designed
transfer mechanism. When that person takes a vacation, the client
relationship pauses. When that person retires, the client relationship
ends. The firm knows this is happening. It does not have a designed
system to prevent it. Signal identifies the constraint: the client
knowledge capture gap, costing the firm estimated revenue from client
attrition at senior transition points. Source maps what the knowledge
is, who holds it, in what form, and what would be lost if the holder
left today. Design allocates the capture work: which knowledge can be
documented by the practitioner using agent-assisted tools, which
knowledge requires structured knowledge transfer sessions with a second
practitioner, and which knowledge requires client-facing discovery to
verify that what the firm believes about the client is what the client
actually believes about themselves. Build deploys the knowledge capture
workflow --- an agent-assisted transcript and synthesis system that
turns every client call into a structured knowledge record without
adding meaningful work to the practitioner\'s day. Deliver trains the
senior practitioners on the handoff: what they do at the end of every
client call changes by three minutes. The agent does the rest. Compound
measures the result against the succession risk baseline established in
Signal and produces a knowledge map that now exists outside any single
person\'s head.]{.underline}]{.insertion author="Julie Mann"
