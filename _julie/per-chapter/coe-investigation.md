This is presented as if COE-as-such carries that four-step structure. It does not (see §4). What she's describing is the operating cadence of one specific employer's COE implementation — a sample of one — generalized as if it were the COE model itself.

She slips "commit" in for Compound without claiming COE has it. So even her own redline does not actually source a four-step COE framework — only a three-step one — and the Compound version adds "commit" on top.

**Bottom line on the redline:** the COE label is real, but the framework presentation is muddled, employer-attributed, and over-claims that COE governance carries the four-step structure. The redline is not strong enough as a standalone source.

## 4. Standard HR / org-design literature

COE is **genuine standard vocabulary** in HR and org-design, with the central authority being Dave Ulrich's HR Business Partner Model.

### Primary authoritative source: Dave Ulrich

> "The HRBP model was first introduced in 1996 by Dave Ulrich and is also referred to as the Ulrich HR model. Dave Ulrich's book *Human Resource Champions* (1997) popularized HR's four roles… By the mid-2000s, Ulrich had evolved the four-role model into the three-pillar structure: HRBPs (strategic partners embedded with business units), Centers of Excellence (expert teams designing programs), and Shared Services (operational delivery at scale). It is often depicted as a 'three-legged stool' (HRBP, COE, Shared Services) supported by enabling technology, governance, and data/analytics."
> — *HRM Handbook, "Dave Ulrich's HR Model"*

> "Centres of expertise are relatively small teams of experts with specialist HR knowledge who work together to develop and promote best practices in their area of expertise, including learning and development, compensation and benefits, employee relations, organisation development, change management, and recruitment."
> — *AIHR, "HR Business Partner Model: A Complete Guide"*

### Consulting / org-design corroboration

> "Specialist capabilities can be structured as centers of excellence within operating models. Governance starts by identifying constituent groups, their meeting rhythm (frequency), primary responsibilities and information reporting."
> — *Deloitte, "Aligning operational governance with the business model"*

> "An OD Center of Excellence (CoE) is a specialized team dedicated to optimizing how a business operates, evolves, and adapts in a dynamic environment, focusing on seamlessly connecting business strategy with organizational capability, leveraging analytics for continuous improvement, and orchestrating high-impact design initiatives."
> — *Institute OD, "A Systems Approach to Creating an OD Center of Excellence"*

> "The Ulrich model, which combines COEs with HRBPs and HR Shared Services, represents the dominant HR operating and organizational model."
> — *Wowledge, "How to Design HR COEs"*

### Findings on the four-step structure

**The four-step "review → validate → commit → update governance" structure is NOT canonical to COE in the literature.** Searches surfaced:

- General governance cadence guidance: "When measuring CoE success becomes part of quarterly business reviews, the CoE transitions from a governance layer into a strategic growth platform." (Aeries Technology)
- Standards-update rhythm: "Standards are reviewed quarterly and adjusted based on effectiveness data." (Adaptive SAG)
- KPI cadence: "Review performance quarterly and link improvements to enterprise-level outcomes." (ANSR)

None of these surface a canonical four-step. COE in the literature is described in terms of **structure (govern / serve / enable)**, **placement (centralized vs. decentralized)**, and **operating rhythm (quarterly reviews common but not prescribed at the structural level)**. The four-step quarterly agenda in Ch 10 is a Compound original; mapping it to COE language is Julie's contribution, not borrowed from the literature.
