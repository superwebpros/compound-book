## Instrument 1 — Two-Column Role Sort (+ ownership material, Action Step, Project Coordinator sort table)

**Registered slug:** `two-column-role-sort`
**Proposed destination:** Designing the Work

### 1a. Moves block (verbatim, with markers)

```
<!-- moves:two-column-role-sort -->
### How to run the *Two-Column Role Sort*
1. **Pick a real role** — Grounds the sort in actual work rather than abstract categories; the job description is not the role as it runs.
2. **List the work as it actually happens** — Capturing what the person spends their week on (not the job description) surfaces the tasks that are ripe for redesign.
3. **Sort each task into Column A or Column B** — Column A = judgment that changes based on context a machine cannot observe; Column B = processing that follows rules a machine could learn.
4. **Split compound tasks** — Many tasks contain both a judgment component and a rule-following component; splitting them prevents undercounting Column B.
5. **Read the completed sort** — The distribution of tasks tells you where the design opportunity is and whether a redesign conversation is unavoidable.
<!-- /moves:two-column-role-sort -->
```

### 1b. Ownership material — "Humans own outcomes. Agents own *tasks*." (verbatim)

The heading this material lives under:

`## Humans own outcomes. Agents own *tasks*.`

Humans own outcomes. Agents own tasks, and the intermediate outputs those tasks produce.

The way we use "ownership" here is specific. It doesn't mean who is aware of a task or who is cc'd on the outcome. It means who is accountable for the decision, the quality, and the next move if something goes wrong. A human always holds that ultimate accountability for the outcome. A human can delegate the *tasks* underneath the outcome to an agent or a team of agents. Each task needs a single named owner. One person, or one agent, picks it up and doesn't put it down until the handoff. But the agent's ownership is always derived from a human's delegation, and the outcome stays on the human's name. Organizations hold humans responsible for outcomes, even in fully automated processes.

Separate the work into two kinds: things with a finish line and things that are ongoing. Some responsibilities have a finish line: a draft gets written, an analysis gets delivered, a campaign gets published. Others are ongoing: the human owns the outcome across time, coaches the system as it learns, and is accountable if the work drifts. Most knowledge work falls apart because the two get treated the same way.

Here is what that looks like in practice.

Compound runs a weekly content cycle. At the start of the week, our marketing lead has decided what we're publishing — which topic, which angle, which channel. That decision is hers. Not the agent's. The agent doesn't know what conversation she had at a conference last Thursday, or what landed badly with the audience two weeks ago, or which piece of content our best clients have been forwarding to their own teams. She does.

Once she decides, the agent takes the brief and produces the first draft: copy, visual direction, distribution plan. She doesn't write the first sentence. She reads the output, marks what is right and what is wrong, and the agent revises. The draft never leaves her without her judgment on it. She owns the outcome. The agent owns the output that precedes it.

When output and outcome get confused, the outcomes drift. That happens when the agent is treated as the decision-maker, or the human is kept inside the execution. That's how you get AI slop, and sometimes real damage. There are gray areas. Some tasks legitimately flip the columns: the agent surfaces decisions (themes from a transcript, patterns in a dataset) and the human executes the creative work (designing brand artifacts, writing the narrative). The principle isn't rigid for every task. What matters is that you decide the split deliberately.


| The Human owns the operating responsibility of | The Agent owns the operating responsibility of |
|---|---|
| Deciding what's worth solving | Research, drafting, first-pass analysis |
| Directing the work; pricing the cost | Decision support and recommendations |
| Owning the outcome and the risk | Documentation, capture, monitoring |
| Coaching the system over time | Executing the routine, on call, on time |

The human side is almost entirely verbs of judgment and direction: *decides, directs, prices, owns, coaches*. The agent side is almost entirely verbs of execution and capture: *drafts, recommends, documents, monitors, executes*. The human directs the agents rather than executing the work faster. That's the shift that makes the math work.

Read those two columns as the structural logic of the model, not just a sort. Agents execute inside the scope a human designed for them. They don't choose what's worth solving in the first place. That call stays with the human, because the agent doesn't know what business you're in, what your reputation is worth, or what a bad week feels like with your largest customer. A company that keeps the human inside the documentation and the first-pass drafting has wasted the model in the other direction. That work has just moved sideways at high cost.

Julie spent years watching executive teams hand decisions off to process layers and lose exactly this: the relational and contextual judgment that no system can hold, that only the person inside the work can supply. You can automate the research, the drafting, the documentation, and the follow-up. You can't automate the judgment that depends on reading the people in the room.

:::{.callout-tip}
## Pro Tip
If you're not sure which column a task belongs in, ask: would I trust a well-briefed new hire to do it competently within 30 days? If yes, it can probably be an agent task. The things that take years to learn (reading a room, knowing when a client is about to churn, choosing which battle to fight) stay with the human.
:::

The ownership table is a design instrument, not a philosophical exercise. The way to learn it is to run it: pick a real role, sort it task by task, and read what the sort tells you.

Elena, the CEO at Meridian Manufacturing, ran this sort on her client-success coordinator role during their first Sprint. The coordinator spent most of her week fielding client status requests, scheduling touchpoints, and chasing internal teams for updates. Most of that work felt relational but wasn't: it followed fixed rules. When Elena sorted the tasks, Column B held more than half the role: scheduling, status emails, update logs, follow-up sequences. Column A held the three things that actually required knowing the client: reading when a relationship was at risk, deciding whether to escalate, and delivering the message when something had gone wrong. Elena hadn't planned to redesign that seat. She ran the sort expecting to confirm the obvious. What she got instead was 58% Column B and a redesign conversation she could no longer avoid.

### 1c. Action Step: Map One Role Against the Two Columns (verbatim, incl. Project Coordinator sort table)

:::{.callout-note}
## Action Step: Map One Role Against the Two Columns
Pick one role in your company — yours, or a direct report's. Think through what that person actually spends their week on (the work as it happens, not the work as the job description writes it). Then sort each task into Column A (Human Intelligence: judgment that changes based on context a machine can't observe) or Column B (Agent Intelligence: processing that follows rules a machine could learn). Some tasks will split. "Managing scope change requests" includes reading client politics (Column A) and updating the change log (Column B). Split those and sort each part.

To make that concrete, here is the sort run on a typical professional services role: a Project Coordinator at a mid-sized firm. (Illustrative composite, not a specific client.)

| Column A (Human Intelligence) | Column B (Agent Intelligence) |
|---|---|
| Managing scope change requests (reading client politics, judging which changes to push back on) | Scheduling client and internal meetings |
| Coordinating between client and delivery team (navigating competing priorities, reading tension) | Sending weekly status update emails |
| Negotiating timeline adjustments (judgment on what to concede, what to hold) | Tracking deliverables against timelines |
| Handling client escalations (de-escalation, relationship repair, knowing when to involve leadership) | Writing meeting summaries and distributing action items |
| | Flagging overdue items to project managers |
| | Updating scope change logs (dates, costs, approvals) |
| | Compiling lessons-learned documents from project notes |

Column B accounts for roughly 55% of this coordinator's week. At a loaded annual cost of $65,000, that is $36,000 per year of human intelligence allocated to work that follows learnable rules. Run the same sort on the role you picked. You'll do the cost calculation a few pages from now.
:::

## Instrument 2 — Calculate the Design Opportunity (cost math)

**Registered slug:** `calculate-design-opportunity`
**Proposed destination:** Designing the Work

### 2a. Moves block (verbatim, with markers)

```
<!-- moves:calculate-design-opportunity -->
### How to calculate the *design opportunity*
1. **Count the tasks in each column** — Establishes the ratio of human-intelligence work to agent-intelligence work for this role.
2. **Estimate hours per week for each task** — Converts task count into time, making the misallocation concrete rather than just a fraction.
3. **Calculate the Column B percentage of the role's week** — Produces the headline number — the share of the role that could run on agent intelligence instead of human hours.
4. **Write the single summary sentence** — 'This role is __% Column B' creates a decision-forcing statement the team can react to.
5. **Multiply Column B percentage by the role's loaded annual cost** — Translates the percentage into an annual dollar misallocation — the cost of not redesigning the role.
<!-- /moves:calculate-design-opportunity -->
```

### 2b. Action Step: Calculate Your Design Opportunity (verbatim, incl. $36K/yr Project Coordinator math)

:::{.callout-note}
## Action Step: Calculate Your Design Opportunity
This step builds on the sort you ran a few pages back. Take the Column A / Column B sort you did for the role you picked. Count the tasks in each column. Estimate the hours per week for each. Calculate the percentage of the role's week that lives in Column B. That is your design opportunity, the share of the role that could be running on agent intelligence instead of human hours.

Write it as a single number: "This role is __% Column B."

*Meridian example:* Elena ran the sort on her client-success coordinator. The number came back 58% Column B. "This role is 58% Column B." That single number started the redesign conversation.

Now multiply that percentage by the person's loaded annual cost (salary + benefits + overhead). That number is the annual operating cost of not redesigning this role.

For the illustrative Project Coordinator: 55% Column B at $65,000 loaded cost = $36,000/year spent on work that follows learnable rules. That is the dollar value of human intelligence being spent on agent-intelligence work.
:::

## Instrument 3 — Map the Hybrid Split (+ Action Step, hybrid-split table)

**Registered slug:** `map-hybrid-split`
**Proposed destination:** Designing the Work

### 3a. Moves block (verbatim, with markers)

```
<!-- moves:map-hybrid-split -->
### How to map the *hybrid split*
1. **Take the completed Column A / Column B sort** — The sort is the raw material; the hybrid split translates it into a redesigned operating picture.
2. **Move Column B tasks into the 'Agent Labors' column** — Names the specific work the agent will own, making the delegation explicit rather than implied.
3. **Move Column A tasks into the 'Human Manages' column** — Names the judgment and direction responsibilities the human retains, clarifying what the redesigned role actually requires.
4. **Add a quality-standard row for the human** — Ensures the human's review responsibility over all agent output is explicit in the design — this is what prevents AI slop shipping under the role's name.
5. **Read the hybrid split as a role description** — The completed table is the shape of the redesigned seat — enough to see whether it is viable and to hand off to Sprint design work.
<!-- /moves:map-hybrid-split -->
```

### 3b. Action Step: Start Your Hybrid Split (verbatim, incl. hybrid-split table)

:::{.callout-note}
## Action Step: Start Your Hybrid Split
Now that you've split the role into Column A and Column B, map the hybrid version: who does what when human and agent share the role. The human manages; the agent does the labor that follows rules.

Here is the illustrative Project Coordinator's hybrid split:

| Human Manages | Agent Labors |
|---|---|
| Scope change requests (reads client politics, judges which changes to push back on) | Schedules client and internal meetings |
| Client-delivery coordination (navigates competing priorities, reads tension) | Sends weekly status update emails from templates |
| Timeline negotiations (decides what to concede, what to hold) | Tracks deliverables against timelines and flags overdue items |
| Client escalations (de-escalation, relationship repair, knowing when to involve leadership) | Writes meeting summaries and distributes action items |
| Quality standard for all agent output — reviews before anything ships | Updates scope change logs (dates, costs, approvals) |
| | Compiles lessons-learned documents from project notes |

This hybrid split is enough to see the shape of the redesigned role. The Source chapter introduces the full design instrument: the **TML framework** (Task / Management / Leadership), which sorts work into three categories rather than two; for now, the two-column version is the right starting point.

If you've got multiple roles touching the same constraint, repeat this process for each one. Compile the results into a single table: Role, Column B %, Loaded Cost, Annual Misallocation Cost. That table is the raw material for the Sprint design work in the chapters ahead.
:::

## Instrument 4 — The Hybrid Accountability Chart (HAC) — full section

**Registered slug:** *(none — no `moves:` block registered for this section)*
**Proposed destination:** Designing the System *(to confirm)*

### 4a. Section (verbatim)

`## The Hybrid Accountability Chart names every *owner*.`

The sort you just ran is the foundation of a more formal instrument called the **Hybrid Accountability Chart** (HAC). The HAC does for the whole org what the two-column sort does for a single role. Every entry maps an accountability against the human-agent split, names one human supervisor for each agent-handled function, and tracks the review cadence. (If you run EOS, the HAC extends your Accountability Chart — it adds agent-handled functions, named supervisors, and review cadences the standard chart doesn't track. You don't replace the chart; you build on top of it.)

Every entry in the HAC follows one rule: there is exactly one named human accountable for every outcome, whether a person or an agent team produces the work. That rule is what prevents the accountability gap we described in the opening story. The agent's work has somewhere to land. Someone's name is on it.

Elena's coordinator sort was one row of Meridian's eventual HAC: one function, mapped against the split, with a named supervisor on the Column B work. The rest of the chart is the same move, repeated across every seat. The Design chapters are where you build the HAC for real: questions, format, completed example. For now, know it exists, and know that the sort you've been doing in this chapter is the raw material it starts from.
