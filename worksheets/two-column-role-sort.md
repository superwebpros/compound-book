# Run the Two-Column Role Sort

*Produces a two-column inventory of one role's weekly tasks — judgment a human must hold (Column A) versus rule-following work an agent could learn (Column B) — so you can see where the redesign opportunity lives.*

This worksheet turns "humans own outcomes, agents own tasks" from a principle into a sorted list for a real seat in your company. You'll pick one role, write down the work as it actually happens, and put every task in one of two columns. No engineer, no ERP, no AI tooling required — you need the name of a role, an honest picture of how that person spends a week, and a pen. The cost math comes later (in *Calculate the Design Opportunity*); the job here is the sort itself.

---

## 1. Pick a real role

Grounds the sort in actual work rather than abstract categories — the job description is not the role as it runs.

1. Name one specific role in your company — yours, or a direct report's. Use the seat, not the person ("client-success coordinator," not "Dana").
2. Pick a role you can picture in detail. The sort only works if you know what the week actually contains, not what the title implies.
3. Write the role name at the top of your sheet. One role per sheet — if several roles touch the same constraint, run a fresh sheet for each.

> **Meridian example:** Elena, the CEO at Meridian Manufacturing, picked her client-success coordinator role during their first Sprint. She ran the sort expecting to confirm the obvious — that most of the work was relational and had to stay human.

---

## 2. List the work as it actually happens

Capturing what the person spends their week on (not the job description) surfaces the tasks that are ripe for redesign.

1. Write down the tasks the role fills a week with — the real cadence of the work, not the duties listed when the job was posted.
2. Be concrete. "Fielding client status requests," "scheduling touchpoints," "chasing internal teams for updates" — each as its own line, not bundled into "client management."
3. Don't judge the tasks yet. Get the full list down first; the sorting happens in the next step. Aim for the dozen-or-so things that genuinely eat the week.

> **Meridian example:** Elena's coordinator spent most of her week fielding client status requests, scheduling touchpoints, and chasing internal teams for updates. Most of that work *felt* relational — but writing it out task by task was what made the next step possible.

---

## 3. Sort each task into Column A or Column B

Column A = judgment that changes based on context a machine cannot observe; Column B = processing that follows rules a machine could learn.

1. Take each task from your list and put it in **Column A (Human Intelligence)** or **Column B (Agent Intelligence)** using the table below.
2. **Column A** = judgment that shifts with context a machine can't see: reading a room, knowing when a client is about to churn, choosing which battle to fight, deciding whether to escalate.
3. **Column B** = processing that follows learnable rules: scheduling, status emails, update logs, follow-up sequences, tracking deliverables, distributing action items.
4. When you're unsure which column a task belongs in, ask the new-hire test: *would I trust a well-briefed new hire to do this competently within 30 days?* If yes, it's probably Column B. If it takes years to learn, it stays in Column A.

> **Meridian example:** When Elena sorted the tasks, Column B held more than half the role — scheduling, status emails, update logs, follow-up sequences. Column A held the three things that actually required knowing the client: reading when a relationship was at risk, deciding whether to escalate, and delivering the message when something had gone wrong.

---

## 4. Split compound tasks

Many tasks contain both a judgment component and a rule-following component; splitting them prevents undercounting Column B.

1. Re-read every task. If a single line contains both judgment *and* rule-following, it's a compound task — break it in two and sort each half.
2. Put the judgment half in Column A and the mechanical half in Column B. Don't let a task's "relational" feel keep its routine half out of Column B.
3. Keep splitting until no line mixes the two kinds of work. Undercounting Column B is the most common way this sort goes wrong.

> **Illustrative example (from the book's Project Coordinator sort):** "Managing scope change requests" splits into reading client politics and judging which changes to push back on (**Column A**) and updating the scope change log with dates, costs, and approvals (**Column B**). Same task, two columns, once you split it.

## 5. Read the completed sort

The distribution of tasks tells you where the design opportunity is and whether a redesign conversation is unavoidable.

1. Look at how the tasks fall across the two columns. Is the week mostly Column B, mostly Column A, or split?
2. Write one sentence naming what the distribution tells you — where this role's design opportunity lives, and whether a redesign conversation just became unavoidable.
3. Carry the sorted table forward. It's the raw material for *Calculate the Design Opportunity*, where you'll turn the Column B share into hours and dollars.

> **Meridian example:** Elena hadn't planned to redesign that seat. What the sort gave her instead was **58% Column B** — and a redesign conversation she could no longer avoid. (For the illustrative Project Coordinator, Column B came to roughly 55% of the week.)

---

## Before you call it done

You're done when someone who wasn't in the room could pick up this sheet and run the cost math without asking you a single question. Check:

- The sheet names one **specific role**, and the tasks listed are the work *as it actually happens* — not the job description.
- Every task sits in exactly one column. No task is left unsorted.
- **Compound tasks are split** — no single line mixes judgment work with rule-following work. (If every Column A entry reads as pure judgment and every Column B entry as pure rule-following, you've split enough.)
- You applied the new-hire test to anything you were unsure about.
- You wrote the one-sentence read of the distribution, not just the columns.
