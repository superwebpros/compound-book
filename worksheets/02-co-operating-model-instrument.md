# Org Mapping Worksheet: Human/Agent Intelligence Split

**Chapter:** 02 — The Co-Operating Model
**Artifact produced:** Role-level intelligence map showing the human/agent split for each role that touches the constraint
**Who's in the room:** Leadership team + the person who owns or occupies the role being mapped

---

## Purpose

This worksheet takes the Co-Operating Model from concept to action. You pick a role, list everything that person does, sort each task into human intelligence or agent intelligence, and calculate the design opportunity. The output is the raw material for the Hybrid Accountability Chart you will build in Design.

---

## Part 1: Pick One Role

Start with one role in the company. If you have already completed Signal and identified a constraint, pick the role closest to that constraint — the person most stretched by it. If you have not done Signal yet, pick whoever is most overloaded, most expensive relative to output, or most likely to quit from grinding work.

Do not pick a department. Pick a person. "Operations" is not a role. "Project Coordinator, Client Services" is a role.

List everything that person does in a typical week. Not what the job description says — what they actually do. Shadow them for a day if you have to. Pull their calendar. Check their sent folder. The task list needs to be honest, not aspirational.

> **Action Step:** Write down 10-15 tasks that one person does weekly. Be specific. "Communications" is not a task. "Writing and sending Monday status emails to 12 active clients" is a task.

---

## Part 2: Sort Into Two Columns

Every task on your list falls into one of two categories.

**Column A — Human Intelligence:** Work that requires judgment, relationship, strategy, taste, negotiation, novel problem-solving, or emotional intelligence. The defining feature: the right answer changes based on context a machine cannot observe.

**Column B — Agent Intelligence:** Work that requires pattern matching, data synthesis, repetitive decisions, status tracking, document generation, scheduling, or research aggregation. The defining feature: the work follows rules a machine could learn.

For each task, ask one question:

*"Does this require judgment that changes based on context a machine can't observe, or does it require processing that follows rules a machine could learn?"*

If the answer is judgment-in-context, it goes in Column A. If the answer is rule-following-at-scale, it goes in Column B.

Some tasks will split. "Managing scope change requests" includes reading the political dynamics of the client relationship (Column A) and updating the change log with dates, costs, and approvals (Column B). Split those tasks into their component parts and sort each part.

> **Pro Tip:** If you are not sure which column a task belongs in, ask: would I trust a well-briefed new hire to do this competently within 30 days? If yes, it can probably be an agent task. The things that take years to learn — reading a room, knowing when a client is about to churn, choosing which battle to fight — those are Column A.

> **Action Step:** Sort the full task list into Column A (Human Intelligence) and Column B (Agent Intelligence). Use a two-column table. Every task from Part 1 must land in one column or be split across both.

---

## Part 3: Calculate the Split

Count the tasks in each column. Estimate the hours per week for each. Calculate the percentage of the role's week that lives in Column B.

That percentage is the design opportunity — the share of the role that could be running on agent intelligence instead of human hours.

Write it as a single number: "This role is __% Column B."

Now multiply that percentage by the person's loaded annual cost (salary + benefits + overhead). That number is the annual operating cost of NOT redesigning this role. It is the dollar value of human intelligence being spent on agent-intelligence work.

> **Pro Tip:** Most operators are shocked by the split. The Column B percentage is usually 40-60% for operational roles. That does not mean you fire the person — it means you redesign what they do. The cost is misallocated intelligence: human hours spent on agent-eligible work.

> **Action Step:** Write the Column B percentage. Multiply by the role's loaded annual cost. That number is the annual cost of running agent-intelligence work on human hours.

---

## Part 4: Imagine the Hybrid Version

If Column B tasks were handled by agents, what would this person do with the freed capacity?

Answer these three questions:

1. Would they do more of the Column A work they already do — but better, deeper, with more attention?
2. Would they take on new responsibilities the organization needs but nobody has time for?
3. Would they manage agent output — reviewing, correcting, and improving the work the agents produce?

The answer is usually all three. This is the Co-Operating Model in miniature: same person, different allocation of intelligence. The role does not disappear. It transforms. The human's time shifts from agent-eligible tasks to the judgment, relationship, and strategy work that justified hiring them.

> **Pro Tip:** The redesigned role is almost always more interesting for the person in it. The work that was grinding them down — the status updates, the data entry, the scheduling back-and-forth — is Column B. The work they are good at and wish they had more time for — the client relationships, the creative problem-solving, the strategic calls — is Column A. You are not taking their job. You are giving them a better one.

> **Action Step:** Write a one-paragraph description of what this role looks like when Column B is handled by agents. Describe a typical day, not a vision statement.

---

## Part 5: Repeat for the Constraint Workflow

Now do this for every role that touches the constraint workflow you identified in Signal. Map each role. Sort each task list. Calculate each split.

When you are done, you will have:

- A list of every role in the constraint workflow
- A Column A / Column B sort for each role
- A percentage split for each role
- A dollar cost of the current misallocation for each role
- A one-paragraph hybrid description for each role

This is the raw material for the Hybrid Accountability Chart you will build in Design. You are not designing solutions yet. You are mapping the terrain so Design has something concrete to work with.

> **Action Step:** Repeat Parts 1-4 for every role in the constraint workflow. Compile the results into a single table with columns for Role, Column B %, Loaded Cost, and Annual Misallocation Cost.

## Populated Example: Project Coordinator at a Professional Services Firm

### Part 1: Task List

1. Scheduling client and internal meetings
2. Sending weekly status update emails to clients
3. Tracking deliverables against project timelines
4. Writing meeting summaries and distributing action items
5. Flagging overdue items to project managers
6. Managing scope change requests
7. Coordinating between client stakeholders and the delivery team
8. Negotiating timeline adjustments when scope shifts
9. Handling client escalations when deliverables are late or off-spec
10. Documenting lessons learned at project close

### Part 2: The Sort

| Column A (Human Intelligence) | Column B (Agent Intelligence) |
|---|---|
| Managing scope change requests (reading client politics, judging which changes to push back on) | Scheduling client and internal meetings |
| Coordinating between client and delivery team (navigating competing priorities, reading tension) | Sending weekly status update emails |
| Negotiating timeline adjustments (judgment on what to concede and what to hold) | Tracking deliverables against timelines |
| Handling client escalations (de-escalation, relationship repair, knowing when to involve leadership) | Writing meeting summaries and distributing action items |
| | Flagging overdue items to project managers |
| | Updating scope change logs (dates, costs, approvals) |
| | Compiling lessons-learned documents from project notes |

### Part 3: The Split

Column B tasks account for roughly 55% of the coordinator's week. Loaded annual cost: $65,000. Annual cost of running Column B on human hours: ~$36,000.

That is $36,000 per year of human intelligence allocated to work that follows learnable rules — scheduling, status reporting, tracking, summarizing, flagging. Every one of those tasks could run on agent intelligence with human oversight.

### Part 4: The Hybrid Version

The redesigned Project Coordinator spends Monday morning reviewing agent-generated status reports and flagging anything that needs a human conversation. The rest of the week goes to the work that actually requires a human: managing two difficult scope negotiations, sitting in on a client call where the relationship is at risk, and coordinating a cross-functional delivery problem that involves competing priorities between three teams. Meeting summaries write themselves. Overdue items surface automatically. The coordinator's calendar is no longer consumed by administrative motion — it is consumed by judgment calls, relationship management, and the kind of coordination that keeps clients from leaving.

## Summary of Action Steps

1. **Pick one role and list tasks** — write down 10-15 tasks that person actually does weekly (Part 1)
2. **Sort into two columns** — Column A (human intelligence) and Column B (agent intelligence) for every task (Part 2)
3. **Calculate the split** — Column B percentage times loaded annual cost equals the annual misallocation cost (Part 3)
4. **Describe the hybrid version** — one paragraph on what the role looks like when Column B runs on agents (Part 4)
5. **Repeat for every role in the constraint workflow** — compile into a single table for Design (Part 5)
