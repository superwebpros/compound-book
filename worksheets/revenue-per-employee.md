# Calculate Revenue per Employee

*Produces a three-row table that shows whether your prior technology investment moved productivity — and whether the AI investment is on track to move it further.*

This worksheet turns the Headcount Paradox from a feeling into a number. You'll capture one productivity figure for three moments — your last major system rollout, today, and a +20%-revenue target with no new hires — and read the delta between them. No accounting system or analyst required: you need two numbers per row (revenue and headcount) and a calculator. Pull last-twelve-months revenue first; everything else divides from there.

---

## 1. Pull last-twelve-months revenue

Establishes the numerator; forces a single agreed-on number before the division.

1. Add up total company revenue for the trailing twelve months (not last fiscal year, not a forecast — the last 12 actual months).
2. Write one number. If two people in the room have two different figures, reconcile before you go further; the whole exercise depends on one agreed-on numerator.
3. Record it in the **Today** row, Revenue column, of the table below.

> **Meridian example:** Elena's team pulled $7.2M in trailing-twelve-month revenue. One number, agreed in the room, before anyone reached for the headcount.

---

## 2. Count current full-time headcount

Establishes the denominator; should reflect payroll today, not budgeted seats.

1. Count full-time employees on payroll **today** — actual people drawing a paycheck, not approved-but-unfilled roles and not budgeted seats.
2. Decide once how you treat part-timers and long-term contractors (e.g., two half-time people = one), and apply that rule the same way in every row.
3. Record the number in the **Today** row, Employees column.

> **Meridian example:** 27 people on payroll today. The two roles the COO had queued for next quarter were *not* counted — budgeted seats are not headcount.

---

## 3. Divide revenue by headcount to get today's baseline

Produces the per-person productivity proxy that all comparisons anchor to.

1. Divide trailing-twelve-month revenue by current headcount.
2. Round to the nearest thousand and write it in the **Today** row, Revenue per Employee column.
3. This single figure is your baseline. Every other row is read against it.

> **Meridian example:** $7.2M ÷ 27 = **$267K** revenue per employee. That's the baseline every other snapshot gets compared to.

---

## 4. Fill in the 'at last major system rollout' row

Gives a before-AI-investment reference point so the prior tech wave's lift is visible.

1. Pick your most recent company-wide system rollout as the anchor — ERP, CRM, PSA, practice-management system, whatever it was. It's the cleanest reference point most operators have.
2. Pull revenue and headcount **as they were at that rollout**, using the same part-timer rule from step 2.
3. Divide and record all three cells in the **At last major system rollout** row.

> **Meridian example:** Four years ago, at the ERP rollout: $5.8M ÷ 24 = **$242K**.

---

## 5. Project the '+20% revenue, no new hires' row

Makes the target slope concrete — shows whether the math is achievable or aspirational.

1. Multiply today's revenue by 1.2 (a 20% increase).
2. Keep headcount **the same as today** — that's the whole point of the row.
3. Divide and record all three cells in the **Next year, +20% revenue, no new hires** row.

> **Meridian example:** $7.2M × 1.2 = $8.6M, still at 27 people: $8.6M ÷ 27 = **$319K**.

## 6. Read the delta across the three rows

Surfaces whether prior investment moved the number and whether the AI investment is on track to move it further.

1. **Rollout → Today:** subtract the rollout figure from today's figure. Did your last big tech investment actually move revenue per employee, and by how much over how many years?
2. **Today → +20% row:** the gap to that row is the productivity lift you'd need to grow 20% without adding bodies. Is that a stretch or a reach?
3. Write one sentence naming what the deltas tell you — e.g., whether prior investment bent the slope and whether the next dollar of revenue still demands the same headcount it always did (the Headcount Paradox).

> **Meridian example:** The ERP rollout moved revenue per employee by **$25K over four years** ($242K → $267K). Elena's single quoting bottleneck — $558K a year in delayed and lost bids — represents more than two years of that prior gain sitting idle in one workflow. The number had barely moved, and the cost of *why* was sitting in a single seat.

> **The principle this number serves:** redesign the operating model before you staff it, and the slope bends. At Jesse's firm, SuperWebPros, that redesign took the company from **thirteen people to eight** — work was mapped function by function, rule-based work moved to AI agent teams, and humans stayed on judgment. Billings went up; profitability went up. That's revenue per employee moving the way it's supposed to.

## Before you call it done

You're done when someone who wasn't in the room could pick up this sheet and understand your productivity picture without asking you a single question. Check:

- All nine cells are filled — three rows, revenue / employees / revenue per employee each. No blanks.
- The same headcount rule (part-timers, contractors) was applied in every row.
- The **Today** figure uses trailing-twelve-month revenue and today's actual payroll — not last fiscal year, not budgeted seats.
- You wrote the one-sentence read of the deltas, not just the numbers.
- You can answer in one breath: did prior investment move this number, and is the next dollar of revenue still pulling the same headcount?
