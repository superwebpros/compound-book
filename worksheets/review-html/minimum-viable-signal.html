Run minimum viable Signal

A three-question seed that turns a vague sense of "something's wrong" into a named, priced, Sprint-solvable constraint.

This worksheet captures the stripped-down, solo version of Signal you can run on yourself today, without an offsite or a facilitated room. You'll leave with one sentence naming your real operational constraint, a number for what it costs you per year, and a clear read on whether a single Sprint can solve it. It's the on-ramp to the full Signal session: the seed here is what the facilitated process later refines. Prerequisite: none — this is the entry point. But do the thinking honestly. The discipline is naming the constraint without rushing to a tool or a solution.

1. Name the operational problem

Forces a specific, observable constraint sentence rather than a vague category — the entry point for every Sprint.

  1. Write one sentence describing a specific, observable problem. Not a department, not a theme — a thing that happens (or fails to happen) in the work.
  2. Apply the category-vs-constraint test: a constraint has a number attached and points at a workflow, a handoff, or a gap. A category doesn't. "Our marketing needs help" is a category. "Our office manager spends three hours a day on manual scheduling coordination" is a constraint.
  3. If your sentence names a person ("Elena is the bottleneck"), rewrite it to name the structural cause ("the quoting knowledge lives only in one person's head"). The fix is a design change, not a hiring or firing decision.
Example — Meridian. The operational problem: quoting took days, and Elena was the only one who could do it. Stated structurally: the quoting knowledge lived nowhere except Elena's head. One sentence, one observable problem, no solution attached yet.

2. Quantify the annual cost

Attaches a dollar/hour/headcount number so the constraint can be prioritized and Sprint ROI can later be measured.

  1. Write a number — dollars, hours, or headcount per year. A rough estimate is fine; precision comes later.
  2. If you genuinely can't estimate it, write that down. That's a finding, not a failure: your first Sprint goal might be to measure it.
  3. Note which lever the cost lives on — lost revenue, wasted hours, a seat you can't fill — so the number is comparable against your other candidate constraints when you rank them.
Example — Meridian. The annual cost: the deals that went cold while a quote sat in Elena's queue. Quoting delay translated directly into lost revenue — a number leadership could feel, which is what made this constraint worth a Sprint over the others on the list.

3. State what would have to be true to solve it

Reveals whether the constraint is Sprint-solvable or whether something upstream must move first, preventing wasted quarters.

  1. Write one sentence describing the condition that, if met, would dissolve the problem.
  2. Read it for solvability. If the condition is something a focused Sprint could build — a workflow, an agent, a redesigned handoff — you have the seed of a Sprint. If it depends on something upstream moving first (a data source that doesn't exist, a decision no one's made), that upstream move is your real first Sprint.
  3. Move the answer into the Active Sprint row of your Hybrid Org Today. If you can't answer it yet, that's your first Sprint: getting to where you can.
Example — Meridian. What would have to be true: the quoting knowledge had to live somewhere other than Elena's head. That condition was buildable in a single Sprint — and it became the seed of Meridian's first one.

Before you call it captured

A colleague who missed every chapter could read your three answers and know exactly what your first Sprint is chasing. Check:

  • The problem is a constraint, not a category — it names an observable thing in the work and would survive the category-vs-constraint test.
  • It names a structure, not a person — the fix is a design change, not a personnel change.
  • There's a number — dollars, hours, or headcount per year (or an explicit "we can't measure this yet, so measuring it is Sprint one").
  • The solve condition reveals the path — you can tell whether one Sprint can solve it or whether something upstream has to move first.
  • It's landed in the Hybrid Org Today — the answer sits in the Active Sprint row, ready to hand to the full Signal process.
