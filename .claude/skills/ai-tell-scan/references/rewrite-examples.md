# Rewrite Examples

Real before/after pairs from AI tell scans. Use these to calibrate rewrite voice, judgment, and the difference between a tell worth fixing and a tell worth keeping.

Genre context for all examples below: practical business worksheets, declarative/unhedged voice, aimed at operators and leadership teams.

---

## Negative parallelism — "not X, it's Y" (two-sentence form)

### Example 1: Triple stack — fix all three

**Before:**
> Decision rights aren't about AI capability. They're about organizational trust. The question isn't "Can AI do this?" — it's "Do we trust the output enough to act on it without a human checking?" That's a leadership decision, not a technology decision.

**Problem:** Three negative-parallelism structures in one paragraph. Each individually is fine; stacked, they read as a template.

**After:**
> Decision rights are a trust question: does the organization trust the output enough to act on it without a human checking? That's a leadership call, and AI capability doesn't answer it.

**Technique:** Collapsed three contrasts into one direct claim. Led with the positive framing.

---

### Example 2: Reframe without the negation

**Before:**
> The human is not the cost. The misallocation of their intelligence is the cost.

**After:**
> The cost is misallocated intelligence: human hours spent on agent-eligible work.

**Technique:** Led with the actual claim. The negation ("human is not the cost") was throat-clearing before the point.

---

### Example 3: Chiastic variant — "stops doing X, starts doing Y"

**Before:**
> The human stops doing what agents should do and starts doing what only humans can do.

**After:**
> The human's time shifts from agent-eligible tasks to the judgment, relationship, and strategy work that justified hiring them.

**Technique:** Replaced abstract categories ("what agents should do" / "what only humans can do") with specific examples. The original sounded like a tagline; the rewrite sounds like a job description.

---

### Example 4: Person-as-constraint reframe

**Before:**
> the constraint is not Sarah. It is the fact that critical knowledge lives in one person's head with no system to distribute it.

**After:**
> the constraint is undistributed knowledge — critical information trapped in one person's head with no system to share it.

**Technique:** Cut the negation, named the constraint directly. The original spent half its words saying what it wasn't.

---

### Example 5: Double pattern — "earned, not assigned" + "not whether... it is whether"

**Before:**
> Autonomy is earned, not assigned. The question is not whether the AI *can* operate autonomously — it is whether you have the governance infrastructure to let it.

**After:**
> Autonomy is earned — the gate is governance infrastructure, not AI capability. Can your monitoring, escalation paths, and review cadence support unsupervised operation?

**Technique:** Kept "earned" (strong verb), collapsed the double contrast into one direct statement, then turned the abstract question into a concrete checklist.

---

### Example 6: "Not X — Y" fragment

**Before:**
> Not a smaller role — a different one.

**After:**
> a role that requires different skills, not fewer.

**Technique:** Integrated into the surrounding sentence rather than standing alone as a dramatic fragment. The fragment form is the tell; the content is fine.

---

### Example 7: Keep a good one

**Before:**
> A sprint is not a committee. It is a small team with direct knowledge of the constraint and the ability to build a solution.

**Decision: Keep.** Strong, definitional, and the only instance in this file. The contrast is real (committees vs. small teams) and the second sentence adds specific information. When thinning the pattern across a collection, keep instances like this where the negation does real work.

## Negative parallelism — triplet variant ("Not X. Not Y. Z.")

### Example 8: Classic triplet buildup

**Before:**
> Not three changes. Not a wish list. One. The change that, applied before the next sprint begins, would have the highest impact on sprint quality.

**After:**
> One change — the one that, applied before the next sprint begins, would have the highest impact on sprint quality.

**Technique:** Cut the two negations entirely. They existed only to create rhythm before the punchline. The punchline stands on its own.

---

### Example 9: Triplet with scene-setting negations

**Before:**
> For each dimension, read the three descriptions and circle the one that matches your company right now. Not where you want to be. Not where you were last quarter. Right now.

**After:**
> For each dimension, read the three descriptions and circle the one that matches your company right now — as it actually operates today.

**Technique:** The negations were reinforcing "right now" — a point the sentence already made. Replaced with a single clarifying phrase.

---

### Example 10: Triple negation before a noun

**Before:**
> A named person. Not a team. Not a department. Not "the AI." A person who owns the output the same way they would own it if a direct report produced it.

**After:**
> A named person — one human who owns the output the same way they would own it if a direct report produced it.

**Technique:** Cut three negations. The specificity of "one human who owns the output" does the work the negations were trying to do.

---

### Example 11: Comma-list collapse

**Before:**
> Before you write the spec, determine which build path fits the design. The design determines the path. Not a vendor relationship. Not an internal preference. Not a market trend.

**After:**
> Before you write the spec, determine which build path fits the design. The design determines the path — not a vendor relationship, an internal preference, or a market trend.

**Technique:** When the negations carry real information (excluding specific wrong approaches), a comma list preserves the content without the staccato buildup.

## Editorializing

### Example 12: "X is the difference between Y and Z"

**Before:**
> If you can't measure what the constraint costs today, you can't measure whether AI made it better tomorrow. Measurement discipline is the difference between an AI initiative and an AI experiment. Experiments are optional. Initiatives have ROI targets.

**After:**
> If you can't measure what the constraint costs today, you can't measure whether AI made it better tomorrow. Without measurement discipline, your AI initiative is an experiment — optional, unmeasured, and easy to kill.

**Technique:** Cut the definitional framing ("is the difference between") and the two follow-up sentences that restated the same point. One sentence does the work of three.

---

### Example 13: "Pretending otherwise"

**Before:**
> Pretending otherwise is why most data audits produce libraries that don't match the business.

**After:**
> Data audits that skip organic sources produce libraries nobody uses.

**Technique:** "Pretending otherwise" is editorial — it tells the reader they're in denial. The rewrite states the consequence directly without the accusation.

---

## Inflated symbolism

### Example 14: Capitalized emphasis as mic-drop

**Before:**
> If your leadership team can't agree on the constraint, that disagreement IS the diagnosis.

**After:**
> If your leadership team can't agree on the constraint, you have your diagnosis: priorities aren't aligned.

**Technique:** Replaced the capitalized IS (manufacturing emphasis) with a colon and the actual diagnosis. The original forced the reader to feel the insight; the rewrite just delivers it.

---

## Clause restating clause

### Example 15: Mirrored structure

**Before:**
> An AI workflow that treats everything as equally current is an AI workflow that will confidently cite your deprecated pricing from 2019.

**After:**
> An AI workflow that treats everything as equally current will confidently cite your deprecated pricing from 2019.

**Technique:** Cut the mirrored phrase. "An X that does Y is an X that does Z" is a structural stutter — the sentence says "AI workflow" twice to create rhetorical weight. Once is enough.

---

## Cross-file pattern density

The most important judgment call in a collection scan is **which instances to keep**. When a pattern appears 15+ times across 11 files:

1. **Keep 3-5 of the strongest** — where the contrast is real, the second clause adds information, and the instance is the only one in its file.
2. **Rewrite the rest** — lead with the positive claim, cut the negation, or collapse into a comma list.
3. **The reader of one file won't notice.** The reader of the whole collection will. Thin for the collection reader.

Patterns kept in the worksheet scan:
- "A sprint is not a committee." (03) — real contrast, specific follow-up
- "The audit is not a formality." (08) — vivid, isolated
- "The operational log is not a complaint box. It is a sensor." (08) — vivid, the metaphor does real work
